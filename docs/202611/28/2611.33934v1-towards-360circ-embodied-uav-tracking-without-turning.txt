Abstract
Conventional embodied UAV tracking couples target observ-
ability with heading control because targets may leave the
field of view of forward-facing cameras. We investigate
a complementary paradigm that uses full-spherical per-
ception to follow designated targets without active head-
ing adjustment.
To support this setting, we introduce
360UAV-ETrack, a simulation benchmark comprising 2,652
sequences, over 1.73 million synchronized observations,
eight tracking tasks, and 150 held-out closed-loop scenarios.

1. Introduction
Embodied UAV tracking requires an agent to continuously
observe and follow a designated moving target. Unlike vi-
sual object tracking in prerecorded videos, embodied track-
ing operates in a closed perception–action loop, where the
agent’s actions influence subsequent observations and its
spatial relationship with the target [20]. Existing approaches
predominantly rely on forward-facing perspective cameras.
Consequently, targets moving to the side or behind the UAV
may leave the field of view (FoV), even when they remain
nearby. Maintaining visual contact thus requires active head-
ing adjustment, coupling target observability with heading.
We investigate a complementary tracking paradigm that
decouples angular target coverage from heading control
through full-spherical perception. As illustrated in Fig. 2,
equirectangular projection (ERP) extends visual coverage
to lateral and rear directions without camera reorientation.
This motivates tracking without turning, in which the UAV
maintains its initial heading and follows the designated target
exclusively through three-dimensional translational control.
While physical occlusions remain unavoidable, full-spherical
perception alleviates target loss caused by limited FoV.
Despite its potential, full-spherical perception remains
underexplored in embodied UAV tracking. Panoramic track-
ing benchmarks such as 360VOT [9] and 360VOTS [25]
focus on offline target localization, whereas embodied ap-
proaches such as TrackVLA [20] and UAV-Track VLA [30]
primarily rely on perspective observations. As summarized
in Table 1, existing benchmarks provide limited support for
full-spherical closed-loop UAV tracking.
To support systematic research in this setting, we intro-
duce 360UAV-ETrack, a large-scale simulation benchmark
built in CARLA. As illustrated in Fig. 1, it provides syn-
chronized panoramic and perspective observations, expert
demonstrations, and diverse tracking scenarios spanning
frontal and non-frontal target configurations. The training
and validation collection comprises 2,652 sequences and
1,733,766 synchronized observation steps, complemented by
150 held-out scenarios for closed-loop evaluation.
However, full-spherical perception introduces several
challenges beyond expanded visual coverage. The wide FoV
increases instance ambiguity, making target identity difficult
to maintain across viewing directions. A single panoramic
observation provides limited evidence of temporal motion,
while ERP distortions and projection discontinuities com-
plicate the translation of spatial relationships into metric
control commands. To address these challenges, we propose

Experiments across two VLA backbones demonstrate
consistent improvements over standard fine-tuning, increas-

of panoramic embodied tracking and motivating further re-

Our contributions are summarized as follows:
• We investigate fixed-heading embodied UAV tracking, de-
coupling angular target coverage from heading control
through full-spherical perception.
• To support this paradigm, we introduce 360UAV-ETrack,
a large-scale benchmark featuring diverse tracking tasks,
expert demonstrations, and closed-loop evaluation.
• We develop 360UAV-TVLA, a reference-conditioned VLA
framework with compressed panoramic memory and
control-aligned trajectory supervision, demonstrating im-
proved closed-loop tracking across two VLA backbones.

2.2. Embodied Visual Tracking
Embodied visual tracking couples target perception with ac-
tive motion control. Early methods improve robustness to
distractors through adversarial learning [1, 17, 31, 32], while
offline reinforcement learning and adaptive policies support
robust tracking across settings [24, 33]. UAV-oriented meth-
ods such as D-VAT [6], DAT [18], and OA-VAT [19] investi-
gate autonomous following and occlusion recovery. Recent
VLA approaches, including TrackVLA [20], UAV-Track
VLA [30], and TrackVLA++ [14], unify target understand-
ing and action prediction; VLM-assisted tracking further
explores failure recovery [23]. CosFly-Track [22] provides
large-scale demonstrations for aerial following. Neverthe-
less, these approaches predominantly use perspective ob-
servations, leaving the role of full-spherical perception in
fixed-heading closed-loop UAV tracking underexplored.
3. 360UAV-ETrack Benchmark
3.1. Simulation and Data Collection
We construct 360UAV-ETrack in CARLA to study full-
spherical embodied UAV tracking under fixed-heading con-
trol. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the UAV follows a designated
moving target through three-dimensional translation without
heading adjustments. The training and validation set con-
tains 2,652 sequences with 1,733,766 ERP frames (48.16
hours), covering eight tracking tasks and 13 weather con-
ditions. Demonstrations are collected across five towns:
Town02, Town05, Town06, Town07, and Town10HD.
The UAV captures 1920 × 960 equirectangular projection
(ERP) observations at 10 Hz, while simulation and low-level
control operate at 30 Hz. Each demonstration provides syn-
chronized panoramic and perspective observations, target
references and annotations, UAV motion states, and expert
velocity commands.
3.2. Tracking Tasks
As shown in Fig. 1, 360UAV-ETrack comprises eight tasks
organized into two evaluation settings.
Task I: Front-Initialized Tracking. Targets are initial-
ized within the frontal 90◦field of view. The four tasks
are (A1) FrontTrack, (A2) FrontUnderpass, (A3) FrontAp-
proach, and (A4) FrontSelection. They evaluate persistent
following, underpass navigation, target approach, and iden-
tity preservation among distractors, respectively.
Task II: Omnidirectional Acquisition and Track-
ing. The four tasks are (B1) NonFrontTrack, (B2) Drone-
Track, (B3) NonFrontApproach, and (B4) NonFrontSelec-
tion. These tasks extend evaluation to non-frontal target
configurations and aerial targets, testing target acquisition
and subsequent tracking under full-spherical observations.
Held-Out Evaluation Set. We construct 150 frozen eval-
uation episodes across Town01, Town03, and Town04, which
are disjoint from the training towns. Task I contains 51
episodes, with 21/7/5/18 episodes for A1–A4. Task II con-
tains 99 episodes, with 44/6/13/36 episodes for B1–B4. The
set includes 72 pedestrian, 72 vehicle, and 6 UAV-target
episodes. Recorded expert demonstrations support offline
replay, while the corresponding frozen scenarios support
online closed-loop evaluation.
3.3. Evaluation Protocol
All policies predict three-dimensional velocity commands
in the same heading-aligned coordinate frame, using the
first predicted command at each decision step. We evaluate
offline prediction and online tracking separately.
Offline Expert Replay. At each timestamp, the policy
receives the recorded expert observation and motion-state
inputs. Predicted velocities are integrated from the expert ini-
tial position with Δ𝑡= 0.1 s, without feeding the integrated
trajectory back into the recorded inputs. We report average
displacement error (ADE) against the expert trajectory, aver-
aging position errors over each complete sequence and then
across episodes. Offline success rate, SRoff, evaluates the in-
tegrated trajectory against the recorded target positions using

4.1. Overall Framework
Full-spherical perception decouples target observability from
UAV heading control, yet poses three fundamental chal-
lenges: (1) how to identify the designated instance within
wide-FoV ERP observations, (2) how to reason about target
dynamics from temporal observations for persistent track-
ing, and (3) how to effectively translate spherical perception
into three-dimensional control. To address these challenges,
we propose 360UAV-TVLA, which integrates three comple-
mentary components: persistent visual referring (PVR, R),
compressed temporal memory (CTM, M), and geometry-
aligned trajectory supervision (GTS, G). As illustrated in
Fig. 3, these components are integrated with a 𝜋-series VLA

4.2. Persistent Visual Referring (PVR)
Full-spherical perception expands target observability but
also increases instance ambiguity, as visually similar objects
may appear across different viewing directions. Language
instructions typically describe target categories rather than
uniquely distinguish individual instances. To maintain tar-
get identity throughout closed-loop tracking, we introduce
persistent visual referring R, which constructs an instance-
specific visual reference from the initial observation:

similar distractors. Only the initial bounding box is provided;
subsequent target coordinates are unavailable to the policy.
4.3. Compressed Temporal Memory (CTM)
While full-spherical perception provides comprehensive spa-
tial coverage, a single observation offers limited evidence
of target dynamics. Persistent tracking requires reasoning
about changes in relative target position and motion direc-
tion. However, directly incorporating historical panoramic
observations substantially increases the visual token budget.
We therefore introduce compressed temporal memory M to
capture instance-specific motion efficiently.

4.5. Joint Learning and Closed-Loop Control
Conditioned on the shared representation 𝑍𝑡, the action
expert of BΘ generates velocity chunks through differen-
tiable reverse-time Euler sampling from Gaussian noise. The
predicted actions are mapped to physical velocities using
training-set normalization statistics and directly supervised
by a masked 𝐿1 imitation loss Lact. We jointly optimize
action imitation and geometry-aligned target trajectory su-
pervision:
L = Lact + 𝜆trajLtraj,
(9)
where 𝜆traj balances velocity imitation and target-motion
learning. Through joint optimization, PVR provides consis-
tent instance conditioning, CTM captures temporal motion
evidence, and GTS encourages control-aligned motion repre-
sentation learning. These complementary components con-
nect target identity, motion understanding, and continuous
velocity control within a unified VLA framework.
At inference, the GTS head is bypassed, and the policy
directly generates velocity commands from the shared repre-
sentation. The controller executes the first predicted veloc-
ity before replanning with updated panoramic observations.
During inference latency, the UAV retains its previous veloc-
ity command while maintaining a fixed heading throughout
tracking.

5.6. Full-Spherical Observation vs. Active Steering
Table 6 compares three observation–control configura-
tions: fixed-heading perspective perception, fixed-heading
panoramic perception, and perspective perception with active
yaw control. With the 𝜋0.5 backbone, replacing perspective
observations with ERP improves ATF by 128.06 and 126.85
frames on Task I and Task II, respectively. These results high-
light the benefits of expanded angular coverage for tracking
persistence and non-frontal acquisition. The evaluated active-
steering policy underperforms both fixed-heading configu-
rations. However, its yaw supervision is synthesized along
recorded translational trajectories rather than obtained from
jointly optimized translation–yaw demonstrations. Thus, this
comparison supports full-spherical perception under the eval-
uated settings without establishing an inherent disadvantage
of active steering.

6. Conclusion
We introduce 360UAV-ETrack, a large-scale benchmark for
full-spherical embodied UAV tracking under fixed-heading
control, supporting both offline expert replay and online
8


closed-loop evaluation. To address the challenges of instance
ambiguity, temporal motion reasoning, and perception-to-
control alignment, we propose 360UAV-TVLA, a panoramic
VLA framework integrating persistent visual referring, com-
pressed temporal memory, and geometry-aligned trajec-
tory supervision. Experiments across two VLA backbones
demonstrate consistent improvements in closed-loop track-
ing and reveal a gap between offline imitation and online
tracking.
These findings highlight the potential of full-
spherical perception for embodied UAV tracking without
active heading adjustment. Future work. We will explore
lightweight models and real-world panoramic UAV deploy-
ment to improve tracking efficiency and robustness.

Protocol (i): Synchronous Closed Loop.
1. At simulation time 𝑡, capture the image and measured
UAV velocity, and start policy inference.
2. Pause the simulator during inference. Neither the UAV

3. Once inference completes, apply the first predicted veloc-

4. Hold this command for 0.1 s of simulated time, then cap-
ture the next observation and repeat.
Thus, inference and external overhead affect experiment wall
time but introduce no delay in simulated time.
Protocol (ii): End-to-End Real-Time Closed Loop.
1. At simulation time 𝑡, capture the image and measured
UAV velocity, and process the observation through the
asynchronous inference pipeline.
2. Keep the simulator running throughout observation prepa-
ration, communication, and inference. The UAV contin-

target continues moving.
3. When the prediction reaches the simulator, apply its first
velocity command at the next available physics update.
4. Hold the new command until the next prediction becomes
effective, repeating the process with updated observa-
tions.
This protocol exposes the policy to the full runtime delay,
including model computation and external overhead.

D. Online Evaluation Protocol
D.1. Task Composition
The evaluation contains 150 episodes: 51 in Task I (front-
initialized persistent tracking; A1–A4) and 99 in Task II
(omnidirectional acquisition and tracking; B1–B4). The set
includes 72 pedestrian, 72 vehicle, and six UAV episodes,
with 50 episodes in each of Town01, Town03, and Town04.
Table 10 gives the task composition and prescribed horizons.
Canonical task categories are used for aggregation; retained
legacy scenario identifiers do not determine the reported
category. Each episode has its own fixed horizon, and every
method is evaluated using the same episode definitions and
thresholds.
D.2. Observations and Target Initialization
All methods receive full-spherical equirectangular observa-
tions from 1920×960 source images; internal preprocessing
follows each method’s specified adapter. The designated tar-
get is identified once in the actual first observation through
a bounding-box annotation, a language instruction, and a
persistent reference image where supported by the adapter.
This initialization remains fixed throughout the episode; sub-
sequent target ground truth is not supplied as per-frame
annotations. The online motion-state input contains the
measured tracker velocity. The Test protocol assigns 75
episodes to zero-speed tracker initialization and 75 episodes
to nonzero-speed initialization. For each nonzero initializa-
tion, the prescribed world-coordinate tracker velocity equals
the target motion reference vector specified by the scenario
controller. This reference vector need not equal the target’s
measured velocity at frame zero during settling or accelera-
tion. The policy receives the tracker velocity measured at its
actual first observation, which may differ slightly from the
prescribed value after the physical initialization steps.
The nominal scenario initialization specifies target bear-
ings of −45◦, 0◦, or 45◦for front-initialized episodes, relative
to the fixed tracker heading. Ground-target Task II scenar-
3


ios specify −135◦, −90◦, 90◦, 135◦, or 180◦; B2 scenarios
specify −155◦or 115◦. These are scenario initialization pa-
rameters; azimuths measured from recorded observations
are computed from the realized tracker and target poses.
A4 and B4 contain one or two explicitly added distractors,
whereas the other categories do not. Distance and visibility
are evaluated against the designated target, not the nearest
distractor.
D.3. Control and Synchronization
Policies output three-dimensional velocities in the fixed
heading-aligned body frame, with zero yaw rate. Obser-
vations and scoring occur at 10 Hz, while the simulator ad-
vances at 30 Hz. Only the first action of each predicted chunk
is executed. Commands whose three-dimensional speed ex-
ceeds 6 m/s are rescaled to this norm while preserving their
direction.
Command timing follows the protocol in Section A. Un-
der synchronous protocol (i), the simulator pauses during
inference, and each new command is held for three physics
ticks (0.1 s). Under protocol (ii), the previous command re-
mains active during the full end-to-end runtime delay. Main-
text online results use protocol (iii): simulated time advances
over the measured model computation interval, while exter-
nal overhead is excluded from simulated delay. The previous
command remains active until the new prediction takes effect
at the first eligible physics boundary. Thus, protocols (ii)
and (iii) do not generally have a fixed three-tick command
hold.
D.4. Visibility, Termination, and Reporting
Target visibility is assessed using simulator occlusion queries
toward multiple points on the target. At least one unob-
structed probe is sufficient for visibility. This geometric
evaluator signal is used only for scoring and is not a model
detection-confidence threshold. Together with the original
distance bounds in Section C, it determines online frame
validity under Eq. (14).
Online episodes stop at the first 15-frame invalid streak
or at their prescribed horizon, as defined in Section B.2. At
10 Hz, the failure tolerance is a nominal 1.5 s window. Runs
interrupted by infrastructure errors are rerun to complete the
evaluation; such interruptions are not reported as completed
successful episodes. SR and ATF are aggregated separately
for A1–A4, B1–B4, Task I, Task II, and the full set. The
complete evaluation uses denominators of 51, 99, and 150
episodes for Task I, Task II, and overall SR, respectively.
Offline expert replay is evaluated separately using ADE
and offline distance SR from Sections B.1 and B.4. Its fixed
observations are not generated by the reconstructed policy
trajectory, so offline distance success does not establish on-
line closed-loop tracking success.
E. Implementation Details of Compared Meth-
ods
All methods are evaluated on the same 150 episodes under
the model-compute-delay protocol. At each decision, the
evaluator executes the first predicted action and holds the
previous velocity while model computation advances the sim-
ulation clock. Translational commands are expressed in the
original initial-heading coordinate frame and are bounded
by a maximum speed of 6 m/s. FPS is computed as the
number of completed inference requests divided by their
total synchronized model-computation time, including re-
quests whose outputs are discarded upon episode termina-
tion. Model loading, warm-up, external image preparation,
communication, rendering, and logging are excluded. For
benchmark-trained models, checkpoint selection uses the
held-out validation split rather than online test performance.
No method receives current ground-truth target states during
deployment.
Uni-NaVid.
We use the released Uni-NaVid-7B check-
point [29] without benchmark fine-tuning or additional train-
able modules. The complete ERP image is letterboxed to the
native 224 × 224 input resolution. Its causal video context is
sampled at 1 Hz and contains at most 32 frames, combining
recent observations with uniformly sampled earlier frames.
The native language interface predicts discrete navigation
commands using deterministic decoding. A fixed adapter
maps forward motion to a longitudinal velocity of 0.5 m/s.
Left, right, and stop commands produce zero translation
because the shared evaluation protocol fixes the UAV head-
ing. These commands are not reinterpreted as lateral motion.
Consequently, this baseline evaluates the released navigation
policy through a fixed interface with restricted UAV control
expressiveness.
OmTrackVLA.
We deploy the released OmTrackVLA-
0.6B checkpoint [11] without benchmark fine-tuning. The
policy receives the language instruction and the latest 32
causal ERP observations, each letterboxed to 384 × 384.
Missing initial history is padded by repeating the earliest
available observation. The native planner predicts eight
planar waypoints with associated yaw values. We convert
successive planar waypoint displacements into horizontal
velocities using a 0.1 s interval, set vertical velocity to zero,
and discard yaw commands under the fixed-heading protocol.
The resulting commands are passed through the common
action executor. No learned UAV action head, target-state
input, or benchmark adaptation is introduced.

We evaluate the published OpenPI

its existing observation and action interfaces. The current

Temporal BC.
Temporal BC is trained from scratch on the
benchmark demonstrations. A shared convolutional encoder
processes the current ERP observation, two preceding obser-
vations at [𝑡−0.2, 𝑡−0.1], and the static target reference. A
256-dimensional gated recurrent unit aggregates the causal
image history, with invalid historical observations masked.
The temporal representation is concatenated with reference
features, encoded UAV velocity, and a target-category code
derived from the instruction. A feed-forward decoder pre-
dicts ten future XYZ velocities. Training minimizes masked
velocity MAE in physical units using AdamW with learning
rate 3 × 10−4, weight decay 0.01, global batch size 64, and
seed 42 for 21,091 updates. The checkpoint with the lowest
held-out validation error is used for closed-loop evaluation.
D-VAT-BC.
D-VAT-BC is a supervised adaptation of the
D-VAT actor architecture [6], rather than an evaluation of
its original reinforcement-learning policy. An ImageNet-
pretrained ResNet-18 encodes the three causal ERP obser-
vations and the static target reference. Historical features
are concatenated and projected before fusion with reference
features, UAV velocity, and the instruction-derived target-
category code. The actor-style multilayer perceptron is re-
tained, while the original control output is replaced with a
decoder for ten future XYZ velocities. The model is trained
with the same masked physical-velocity MAE, optimizer set-
tings, sample budget, and validation selection procedure as
Temporal BC. No reinforcement-learning reward, additional
environment interaction, or online target-state supervision is
used.
Fast-WAM.
We initialize Fast-WAM [27] from its re-
leased two-camera LIBERO checkpoint and fine-tune it on
the benchmark demonstrations. The current ERP observation
and static target reference are encoded through its image-
latent pathway, while the language instruction and normal-
ized UAV velocity provide additional conditioning. The
action branch predicts a ten-step XYZ velocity chunk using
its native iterative sampler with ten sampling steps. Bench-
mark adaptation minimizes the masked physical-velocity
MAE of the sampled actions; it does not introduce future
images as policy inputs or an additional benchmark video
prediction objective. Training uses one epoch, global batch
size 64, peak learning rate 2.5 × 10−5, and seed 42. The
selected policy executes only its first predicted action.

jointly optimizes masked XYZ velocity MAE and the GTS
trajectory loss under the configured loss weight, using the
same data split, training budget, and validation-based check-
point selection. During deployment, CTM maintains a causal
10 Hz history cache, including during simulated computation
delays. The policy executes only the first velocity gener-
ated by native ten-step flow sampling, with the GTS head
bypassed.
F. Expert Demonstration Generation
F.1. Reference Trajectory Generation
Expert demonstrations are generated using task-specific
controllers in CARLA. Pedestrians and vehicles follow
navigable routes, while aerial targets use dedicated three-
dimensional motion controllers. The UAV reference position
is constructed from the target’s bounding-box center, its
observed motion, and the desired following geometry. Track-
ing sequences use target-relative offsets or distance-based
interpolation along the recorded target trail. Where required,
a smooth transition connects the initial offset to the trail-
ing reference. Approach and acquisition tasks additionally
include task-specific initialization phases before entering
sustained following. In multi-target scenes, the reference
remains associated with the designated target.

F.3. Terrain Clearance and Obstacle Handling
Initialization checks exclude UAV poses embedded in scene
geometry and select task-appropriate vertical offsets. During
motion, local geometry queries inspect the commanded flight
corridor, including lateral or vertical clearance margins. Ob-
stacle handling depends on the task: controllers may brake,
try locally checked climbing or lateral commands, or reject
the trial when the prescribed following geometry cannot be
maintained safely. Collision callbacks and additional scene-
specific geometry checks identify invalid segments. Only
segments satisfying the corresponding collection and quality-
control checks are retained. These procedures provide local
safeguards rather than a globally optimal collision-avoidance
planner.
F.4. Demonstration Recording
The simulator advances synchronously at 30 Hz, with
panoramic observations, UAV motion states, target anno-
tations, and expert velocity commands recorded every three
physics ticks at 10 Hz. Sensor frames and actor states are
matched by simulator frame identifiers and timestamps. Is-
sued commands are stored separately from measured UAV
velocities. The UAV maintains its initial heading throughout
each demonstration, with zero commanded yaw rate. Simu-
lator target states support expert generation and annotation;
target trajectories are not supplied to the deployed velocity
policy.

I.4. Timing-protocol sensitivity.
Protocol (i) evaluates synchronized action application by
pausing the simulator during inference. Under this setting,
360UAV-TVLA achieves the strongest overall performance
among the evaluated VLA models. The 𝜋0 variant reaches
394.15 frames, 36.46% normalized ATF, and 12.00% SR,
while the 𝜋0.5 variant reaches 337.95 frames, 33.52% nor-
malized ATF, and 12.00% SR. Both variants outperform their
corresponding fine-tuned baselines in ATF, normalized ATF,
and success rate. Protocol (ii) removes the synchronization
barrier: the simulator continues to evolve during inference
and the UAV holds its previous command until a new ac-
tion is returned. Overall performance decreases to 199.99
frames, 21.08% normalized ATF, and 3.33% SR for the 𝜋0
variant, and to 185.03 frames, 19.12% normalized ATF, and
3.33% SR for the 𝜋0.5 variant. This reduction is attributed to
observation–action staleness caused by continued target and
UAV motion during inference. Nevertheless, both variants
remain above their corresponding fine-tuned 𝜋baselines in
overall ATF and SR.
The two protocols therefore measure complementary
properties. Protocol (i) isolates policy quality under syn-
chronized action application, whereas Protocol (ii) evaluates
robustness to temporal misalignment and delayed action up-
dates. Protocol (ii) follows the continued-world definition
specified for this study; its clock diagnostics are retained
separately and are not used as an additional score-eligibility
criterion.
