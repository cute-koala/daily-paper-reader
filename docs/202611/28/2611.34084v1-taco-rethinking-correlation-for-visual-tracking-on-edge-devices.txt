TaCo: Rethinking Correlation for Visual Tracking on Edge Devices
Qingmao Wei1,2
Fagui Liu1,2,∗
Quan Tang2,∗
1South China University of Technology
2Pengcheng Laboratory
Abstract
Lightweight trackers have replaced cross-correlation with
attention, and on low-power edge hardware they pay for
it, since memory-bound softmax, normalization and re-
shaping operators of attention cost far more time than
their arithmetic suggests.
Benchmarking efficient track-
ers on an edge Neural Processing Unit (NPU) and an
Arm CPU under one numerically validated protocol, we
find that correlation trackers are the fast ones, but they
lag in accuracy unless they adopt a ViT backbone, which
brings attention back. We revisit what correlation lacks.
Template–search interaction serves two roles: condition-
ing the search features on the target and matching them
against it spatially. Attention does both, whereas correla-
tion only matches, and does so on features extracted with-
out knowledge of the target.
We present TaCo (Target-
aligned Correlation), which supplies the missing condition-
ing without attention by letting the template enter the per-
frame network only as operands computed once per video.
A descriptor of the target scales and shifts the search fea-
tures channel by channel inside the backbone, and kernels
aligned with the target box, each with a weight predicted
from the template, correlate with them in one matrix prod-
uct. With the backbone fixed, our correlation remains es-
sential while incurring negligible NPU overhead, offering
a better speed-accuracy trade-off than the attention-based
interactions we tested. TaCo-T achieves 62.3% LaSOT AUC
at 184 FPS on the Rockchip RV1126B NPU and 96 FPS
on a Raspberry Pi 5 CPU, 3.0× and 3.5× the NPU speed
of HiT-Tiny and AsymTrack-T at higher accuracy. TaCo-B
reaches 65.6% at 50 FPS on the NPU, more than twice the
speed of HiT-Base, AsymTrack-B and LightFC-vit, again
at higher accuracy.
Code and models are available at
https://github.com/TsingWei/TaCo-Tracker.

This motivates a closer look at why correlation track-
ers lag behind attention trackers. Interaction between the
template and search features serves two roles, conditioning
search feature extraction on the target and supplying spa-
tial matching evidence for localization. Siamese trackers
extract search features without target knowledge and apply
correlation only near the head [1, 17, 19], whereas rescuing
accuracy with a ViT backbone [19] forfeits edge-friendly
operators. Attention performs both roles in every layer and
couples them inside costly ops, yet the two roles need not
share an operator. We ask whether a correlation tracker can
gain target-aware features while retaining inexpensive ex-
plicit matching on edge devices.
We answer this with TaCo (Target-aligned Correlation),
which separates the two roles across edge-friendly opera-
tors. Using a CNN backbone truncated at stride 16, TaCo
computes all template-derived operands once per video.
A box-weighted descriptor predicts channel-wise scales
and shifts before the stride-16 search blocks, while box-
aligned RoIAlign target kernels, pooled context kernels,
and template-predicted kernel weights separate part-level
foreground matching from background context and cal-
ibrate correlation responses without per-frame overhead.
Because search features are already target-aware, we drop
post-correlation fusion blocks and share compact reparam-
eterizable RepConv layers [8] across the head, folding train-
ing branches into plain convolutions at deployment. Per
frame, the template enters only as operands of channel-wise
multiply-adds and a single matrix product, without softmax,
token normalization, or attention reshapes.
Instantiating TaCo across three CNN variants (TaCo-
T with LowFormer-E1, TaCo-S with RepViT-M1.1, and
TaCo-B with RepViT-M1.5) extends the accuracy and speed
frontier on both devices (Fig. 1). On RV1126B and Rasp-
berry Pi 5, the TaCo family consistently outperforms prior
lightweight trackers across standard benchmarks while scal-
ing smoothly from ultra-fast real-time inference to higher-
capacity models. For instance, TaCo-T reaches 184 FPS on
RV1126B (over 3× faster than HiT-Tiny and AsymTrack-T
with higher accuracy), while TaCo-B attains 65.6% LaSOT
AUC at 50 NPU FPS (2.3× faster than LightFC-vit at higher
accuracy) alongside strong gains across TrackingNet, GOT-
10k, and VastTrack. Controlled ablations confirm that both
decoupled roles are essential, and transplanting this inter-
action onto representative transformer, CNN, and hybrid
backbones (HiT, LightFC, and AsymTrack-T) likewise pre-
serves or improves accuracy at substantially lower edge la-
tency.
We make three contributions:
• Rethinking correlation. A controlled study and trans-
fer analysis across backbones separate target-conditioned
feature extraction from explicit spatial matching, es-
tablishing their complementary gains and measured
NPU/CPU operator costs.
• TaCo, an edge tracking family. We realize this division
using template operands cached once per video (target-
guided modulation, target-aligned kernels with cached
kernel weighting, and a shared reparameterizable head)
across three CNN backbones.
• A unified edge benchmark. We pair compiled RV1126B
NPU and Raspberry Pi 5 Arm CPU graph timings, veri-
fied against FP32 numerical references, with re-evaluated
accuracy across four tracking benchmarks.
2. Related Work
Correlation-based tracking. Siamese trackers match tem-
plate and search representations through explicit cross-
correlation after feature extraction [1, 16, 17]. Pixel-wise
correlation treats template feature vectors as individual 1×1
kernels, producing a similarity map for each template loca-
tion [11, 21, 39]. Lightweight designs reduce the cost of this
pipeline through architecture search [38], compact fusion
modules [2], or streamlined pixel-wise matching [19, 20].
LightFC combines pixel-wise matching with channel at-
tention and convolutional fusion, and its ViT variant uses
a stronger feature extractor while retaining the matching
pipeline [19]. Target-aware feature extraction has also been
2


explored. InBN [12] injects target information into Siamese
backbone stages, while SBT [35] interleaves cross-image
interaction with feature extraction and removes the separate
correlation step. These studies establish the relevance of
where template information enters the tracking network.
Attention-based and efficient tracking. TransT [4] and
STARK [37] use attention to fuse features from convolu-
tional backbones, while one-stream trackers such as OS-
Track [41], MixFormer [6], and SimTrack [3] unify inter-
action between template and search with feature extraction.
To reduce the resulting quadratic cost, efficient variants ex-
plore hierarchical or separable-attention transformers [10,
14, 15, 44], alongside distillation, layer-adaptive or sparse
inference, and fast autoregressive designs [7, 30, 32, 36].
Template caching further avoids repeated encoding through
asymmetric attention [6] or asynchronous feature extrac-
tion [31]. Yet even when template encoding is cached or
theoretical MACs are lowered, search-side softmax atten-
tion, normalization, and heavy token reshapes still execute
every frame. As a result, latency reductions reported on
desktop GPUs or uncompiled PyTorch do not directly trans-
fer to compiled edge runtimes, where non-convolutional op-
erators dominate execution time and can introduce FP16 nu-
merical divergence.
Feature-wise modulation.
FiLM [27] conditions neu-
ral features on external context through per-channel affine
transformations. For video segmentation, OSMN [40] pre-
dicts object-specific channel scales from the first annotated
frame, using a separate modulator to specialize the segmen-
tation network to the object of interest. In tracking, Au-
toMatch [43] evaluates FiLM as a matching operator after
backbone feature extraction and retains it in both classifi-
cation and regression branches. PromptVT [42] introduces
target guidance through visual prompts. AsymTrack [46]
caches template representations and injects them into the
search branch through prototype-based modulation, retain-
ing a later attention layer for interaction. TaCo uses feature-
wise modulation within the search backbone and retains ex-
plicit correlation for spatial matching, evaluating this divi-
sion under the operator costs of edge inference.
3. TaCo: Target-aligned Correlation
3.1. Rethinking correlation
An interaction between a template and search features X ∈
RC×HW has two functions. It makes X target-aware and
matches the positions of X against the target. Trackers have
used three forms of interaction,

attention trackers repeat it through the network, whereas a
correlation tracker applies Eq. (1) once, to the output of a
backbone that never sees the target.
The forms also differ in how the template enters the per-
frame graph. In Eqs. (1) and (3) it is an operand, computed
once per video, of a matrix product or of a channel-wise
multiply-add. Eq. (2) runs, even with cached keys and val-
ues, a softmax, a token normalization and the layout op-
erators between attention heads in every frame, which an
edge NPU executes far below the throughput of convolu-
tions and matrix products (Sec. 4.3). We therefore treat cor-
relation and modulation as complements, each covering the
function the other lacks, under one design rule. We com-
pute the template-derived parameters once per video and
apply them to search features only through channel-wise
multiply-adds and matrix products, without attention soft-
max or token normalization.

3.3. Target-aligned correlation
Modulation tells the backbone what to look for, but it acts
on channels and never compares search positions with the
template, which is what correlation does. Following pixel-
wise correlation [39], we use template feature vectors as
1×1 kernels and correlate them with the modulated search
feature X ∈RC×HW , so the target enters the match from
both sides. Since X already carries the target, we also revise
what the correlation is given and what follows it.
Target-aligned kernels. We sample P×P target cells on
a grid aligned with the box using RoIAlign, and average-
pool Q×Q context cells over the whole template, giving
Nk = P 2 + Q2 = 32 kernels ki ∈RC (P = Q = 4),
scaled by C−1/2. LightFC [19] takes its kernels from a
fixed 8×8 grid over the template crop, so cells mix target
and background wherever the box falls. Pairing box-aligned
target kernels with global context kernels separates part-
level foreground matching from surrounding background
anchors, which is particularly helpful when small targets
cover only a few cells on the stride-16 template grid. Be-
cause the target box has continuous location and aspect ra-
tio on this coarse 8×8 map, RoIAlign uses bilinear interpo-
lation to avoid quantization misalignment and background
leakage. It runs once per video in the template branch and
never enters the per-frame NPU graph.
Context instead
spans the fixed full-template grid and needs no box align-
ment, so 4×4 average pooling aggregates each 2×2 neigh-
borhood into 16 smooth regional prototypes without resam-
pling, avoiding single-peak dominance from max pooling
4


and halving the kernel count relative to all 64 template cells.
Correlation. The response R = KX ∈RNk×HW is a
single matrix product per frame with K as a per-video input,
followed by batch normalization.
Lite kernel weighting.
LightFC reweights the response
channels with a squeeze-and-excitation block, which is
computed from the search response and therefore runs in
every frame. We instead predict the weight of each kernel
from the template, once per video. A shared MLP maps the
normalized feature of ki and the fraction ai of its cell in-
side the box to a scalar gain wi and bias bi, applied to the
normalized response of that kernel:
Ri ←wiRi + bi.
(6)
Target kernels have ai = 1, so the coverage separates target
from context kernels. The last layer is initialized to wi = 1
and bi = 0. The weighted response is reduced to 64 chan-
nels by a 1×1 convolution and concatenated with X as the
input of the head. We drop the fusion layers that LightFC
places at this point (depth-wise convolutions and an inverted
bottleneck), since they add no accuracy once X is target-
aware (Sec. 4.3).
3.4. Head and loss
A centre head [41, 45] predicts the score map ˆS, a sub-
cell offset ˆO and the box size ˆB on the stride-16 grid Ω.
Its layers are RepConv blocks [8], which train with parallel
branches and fold into single 3×3 convolutions for deploy-
ment. Whereas LightFC [19] feeds a post-correlation fusion
block into three separate 256-channel branches, we pass the
concatenated search and correlation features directly into a
narrower 128-channel head whose first two RepConv lay-
ers are shared across all three outputs. Since X is already
target-aware and the correlation projection provides spatial
evidence, sharing a compact 128-channel stem eliminates
redundant per-branch convolutions and saves NPU latency
without losing accuracy, while separate final layers preserve
task-specific score, offset and size predictions.
We train the complete tracker with
L = Lfocal + 2 LGIoU + 5 L1,
(7)
using focal loss [23] on the score map and GIoU [28] and
L1 losses on the decoded box.
4. Experiments
4.1. Implementation details
Model. TaCo-T, TaCo-S and TaCo-B use LowFormer-E1,
RepViT-M1.1 and RepViT-M1.5, respectively. All three use
128×128 templates, 256×256 search crops and stride-16
output features. They share the correlation and head de-
signs, with 4, 13 and 25 modulation sites in s3, respectively
(see Tab. 7).
Training. We initialize each backbone with ImageNet clas-
sification weights and sample 60,000 image pairs per epoch,
drawing equally from the training splits of LaSOT [9],
GOT-10k [13], COCO [22] and VastTrack [26]. We train
for 300 epochs on four V100 GPUs with AdamW, a global
batch size of 128 and the tracking loss in Eq. (7). The initial
learning rates are 8×10−5 for the backbone and 8×10−4 for
the remaining parameters, both reduced by a factor of ten at
epoch 240.
Inference. We crop the template around the first-frame an-
notation and cache the modulation parameters, correlation
kernels and kernel weights for the sequence. At each subse-
quent frame, we crop the search region around the previous
prediction, weight the score map with a Hann window and
decode the box from the offset and size at the peak. The
template and search crop factors are 2 and 4, respectively.

4.3. Controlled study on rethinking correlation
Tabs. 3 to 5 report averages over two seeds (200 epochs,
128×128 template, 256×256 search) and latency over 256
crops on RV1126B NPU and Pi 5 Arm CPU. Without target
alignment, the 16 target kernels revert to a fixed template
grid.
Is correlation needed, and what does it cost? Explicit
correlation is the largest single accuracy factor and nearly
free on edge hardware (Tab. 3). Removing it costs 2.48
LaSOT and 1.18 VastTrack AUC but saves only 0.20 ms
on the NPU and 0.68 ms on Arm. Cross and joint atten-
tion in its place lose 2.9–3.5 LaSOT and 6.6–7.1 VastTrack
AUC at 1.4–1.7× the NPU and 2.3–2.8× the Arm latency.
The VastTrack loss is about twice the LaSOT loss, suggest-
ing that a learned attention readout generalizes worse than
the built-in 2D correspondence of correlation to VastTrack’s
wider range of classes.
What does correlation lack, and where to modulate? Re-
moving target-guided modulation costs 0.59 LaSOT and
0.77 VastTrack AUC, while the modulation takes only
0.12 ms on the NPU and 0.11 ms on Arm, under 2.3% of
frame time (Tab. 3). Among placements (Tab. 4), modu-
lating all three stages gains only 0.12 LaSOT AUC over
s3 alone, trails it in LaSOT precision and on VastTrack,
and adds 0.48 ms on the NPU and 0.74 ms on Arm on 16×
and 4× larger early maps. Modulating only before the four
stride-16 s3 blocks gives the best precision on both bench-
marks at nearly the lowest latency. A channel-wise scale se-
lects whole channels, which we expect to matter most where
channels encode object parts rather than low-level edges.
How should correlation adapt to target-aware features?
Because its operands are cached once per video, the up-
graded correlation costs almost nothing per frame (Tab. 3).
Target alignment adds 0.73 LaSOT and 0.24 VastTrack
AUC at the same NPU latency, and lite kernel weighting
adds 0.62 and 0.11 for 0.01 ms.
In Tab. 5, target align-
ment helps only with lite kernel weighting and lowers AUC
with SE (61.50 to 60.83), likely because SE, computed from
the search response, cannot tell context kernels from target
kernels, whereas lite kernel weighting receives each ker-
nel’s box coverage ai.
Dropping the fusion layers then
gives the highest AUC in the table (61.55) at the lowest la-
tency, 0.57 ms (9.5%) faster than LightFC’s correlation on
the NPU and 0.66 ms faster on Arm.

5. Conclusion
We set out to rethink correlation for edge tracking, and
on-device measurements change what should be asked of
it. On edge NPUs and Arm CPUs, explicit correlation is
nearly free and essential for accuracy, whereas attention’s
softmax, normalization, and layout ops incur heavy latency
concealed by MAC counts. What correlation lacked was a
target-aware search backbone, supplied by cached channel-
wise scale and shift at a fraction of attention’s cost. TaCo re-
alizes this division via target-guided modulation and target-
aligned correlation with cached per-video operands, trans-
ferring across backbones and reaching 184 FPS at 62.3%

A. Architecture details
All three models use a shared template/search backbone and
stride-16 output features. The template and search inputs
are 128 × 128 and 256 × 256, producing 8 × 8 and 16 × 16
terminal grids. The stride-32 stage is omitted in the three
deployed CNN variants. A box-pooled template descriptor
generates a channel-wise scale and shift before each block
of s3. There are 4, 13 and 25 modulation sites in TaCo-T,
TaCo-S and TaCo-B, respectively. The template branch is
evaluated without these modulations.
The correlation cache contains 16 kernels pooled inside
the target box and 16 pooled over the template crop. Ker-
nel weighting uses the feature of each kernel and the frac-
tional overlap of its cell with the target box. The corre-
lation response is normalized, weighted kernel by kernel
and reduced to 64 channels by a 1×1 convolution before
concatenation with the search feature. The fusion layers
of LightFC are not used. The 128-channel RepConv cen-
tre head shares its first two layers between the score, offset
and size branches. Parameter counts, per-frame MACs, and
both per-frame and one-time template-branch latencies are
in Tab. 7.

ence, the correlation branch uses the running mean and vari-
ance accumulated during training, along with the learned
BatchNorm affine parameters. For each response channel i,

Kernel responses. A response map is eRi after batch nor-
malization and kernel weighting (Eqs. (6) and (8)) on the
stride-16 search grid. Each of the 32 maps is standardized
over its interior cells (excluding the outermost ring). The
maps of the 16 target kernels, or of the 16 context kernels,
are then averaged, and the signed mean is drawn after bi-
linear upsampling using a shared symmetric scale for all
target and context tiles in each figure. Red and blue denote
positive and negative standardized responses, respectively,
with zero corresponding to each kernel’s spatial mean. The
outermost cells are not drawn, because the corner cell car-
ries a padding artifact of the backbone that would set the
scale. The centroid of a kernel row is the centroid of the
positive part of the mean map of its four kernels, over the
cells whose centre lies in the predicted box enlarged by half
a cell.
Mean modulation operands. The mean scale and shift of
every modulation site are taken over the 243 sequences. To
isolate per-frame selectivity without accumulating closed-
loop drift, each search crop selected by the unmodified
tracker is evaluated a second time with the mean operands,
and both predictions are compared with the ground truth
on the 162,444 annotated frames. Even in this open-loop
single-frame re-evaluation, where errors cannot propagate
across frames, mean IoU drops from 0.679 with the tar-
get’s operands to 0.677 with the mean operands. We count
a frame as losing the target when one prediction has IoU be-
low 0.2 while the other is above 0.5: this happens with the
mean operands on 175 frames versus 70 frames with the tar-
get operands. The last two rows of Fig. 7b show examples
of the second kind.
