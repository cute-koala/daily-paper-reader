Tracking Objects Through Assembly and Decomposition
Cheryl Lam
Yihong Sun
Bharath Hariharan
Cornell University
Abstract
Tracking objects through transformations is essential for un-
derstanding real-world dynamics. Recent work has extended
traditional object tracking to detect state changes. However,
existing methods require an initial object prompt and de-
tect only its decompositions, missing assembly events, which
begin with multiple objects rather than one. We therefore
introduce a prompt-free formulation of this task, where the
goal is to recover all object tracks and detect both decom-
position and assembly events given only a video. Our key
insight is that assembly is decomposition in reverse. Build-
ing on this insight, we present ATLAS, a zero-shot system
that constructs and jointly reasons over forward and reverse
spatiotemporal partitions to recover object tracks and their
transformations. We further introduce OMNIST, a bench-
mark with dense annotations of objects and both transforma-
tion types. On OMNIST, ATLAS recovers more than twice as
many state changes as prior methods while remaining com-
petitive on existing benchmarks that only evaluate decompo-
sition. Code, data, and additional results are available at
https://atlas-track-objects.github.io/.
1. Introduction
Object transformations are everywhere: a chef slices a
tomato; a child assembles a toy; a caterpillar becomes a
chrysalis and later emerges as a butterfly. Understanding
these changes is crucial, whether for reasoning about pre-
and post-conditions of actions by embodied agents [20, 28],
monitoring wildlife across developmental stages, or creating
policy sub-goals from recorded demonstrations [15].
One approach is to ask a multimodal language model
(MLLM) to identify transformations in a video [1, 33]. Un-
fortunately, MLLMs provide only coarse descriptions of
what occurred. Mounting evidence suggests that current
MLLMs struggle to spatiotemporally ground temporal events
in videos [9, 43]. This lack of grounding is problematic: e.g.,
it prevents embodied agents from tying transformations (“a
boy building a toy”) to the specific objects and context (the
particular pieces and how they go together).
To ground transformations in the video, one must also
track the objects involved. Recently, Sun et al. [31] extended
traditional object tracking to detect state transformations as
well. Their approach, TubeletGraph, takes a video and an ini-
tial target object mask as input, tracks the object through its
transformations, and produces a state graph that records each
transformation along with its timing, participating objects,
and natural-language description.
However, this formulation has a critical flaw. Like tradi-
tional object tracking, the problem is formulated as tracking
a single object annotated in the first frame. This limits
TubeletGraph [31] and other current systems [30] to de-
composition events, where the tracked object fragments
into independently trackable parts. When the reverse oc-
curs—multiple objects merging during assembly—these sys-
tems simply fail to detect the event, either silently ending the
track or continuing it through the assembled object, result-
ing in incomplete and misleading output. Beyond assembly,
current systems also miss any further decompositions of the
fragments from an earlier decomposition since they only
reason about the transformations of the original prompted
object. For example, in Figure 1, TubeletGraph detects the
removal of the bag from the box, but misses the subsequent
decomposition (removing crackers from the bag) and assem-
bly (inserting the bag back into the box).
To truly track any transformation, we must relax the tra-
ditional tracking assumption of a single “prompt” object and
instead account for all objects in the scene. Concretely, given
only a video, the goal is to produce a spatiotemporal state
graph recording every decomposition and assembly along
with its timing, participating objects, and natural-language
description. This increases task difficulty, as one must now
search for all objects that may undergo a state change without
any prompt to anchor the search.
To solve this, we present ATLAS (All-object Tracking
and Localization Across State changes), a zero-shot frame-
work for prompt-free tracking and understanding of object
transformations. Our key insight is that assembly is de-
composition in reverse: a video of objects merging is, up
to temporal direction, indistinguishable from one in which
an object splits. Based on this insight, ATLAS constructs
forward and reverse spatiotemporal partitions and jointly

which start from multiple objects instead of one prompt

In sum, the contributions of our work are:
1. We introduce the task of tracking all objects and detecting
their transformations given only a video.
2. We propose ATLAS, a zero-shot framework that requires
no initial prompt, constructs bidirectional spatiotemporal
partitions, and models assembly as the temporal reverse
of decomposition.
3. We introduce OMNIST, a benchmark with decomposition
and assembly events, and demonstrate state-of-the-art
performance on OMNIST while matching prior art on
existing benchmarks.
2. Related Work
Object Tracking and Transformation Understanding. Ob-
ject tracking methods [16] typically segment a target object
specified by an initial mask and propagate it forward via
appearance features [2, 3, 6, 8, 14, 25]. SAM2 [27] sub-
stantially advances this paradigm with a streaming memory-
attention mechanism. Extensions such as SAM2Long [11],
SAMURAI [39], and DAM4SAM [35] address error ac-
cumulation, crowded scenes, and visual distractors. How-
ever, under dramatic visual changes, these trackers lose the
target object. Datasets such as VOST [34], VSCOS [41],
DTTO [37], and M3-VOS [5] benchmark this challenge. Re-
VOS [5] proposes combining forward and backward memory
to improve robustness through transformations, while recent
approaches improve SAM2 during state transformations, but
require training [10]. However, these methods do not recover
the transformations themselves. TubeletGraph [31] extends
object tracking by introducing Track Any State: given an
initial mask, it recovers objects missed after decomposition
via a spatiotemporal partition and constructs a state graph
through VLM reasoning. Our work directly builds on Track
Any State, but removes the initial prompt requirement and
extends the framework to handle assembly transformations.
Beyond prompt-based tracking, prompt-free and panoptic
video segmentation methods [7, 18, 40] track all objects in
a scene simultaneously without a per-object prompt. How-
ever, these methods do not model transformations: splits
2


and merges are treated as the appearance of new objects
rather than state change events. Our method is the first to
combine prompt-free all-object tracking with transformation
understanding.
Object State Change Recognition. A complementary line
of work studies object state changes without maintaining
persistent object tracks. HowToChange [38] and Where-
ToChange [24] train models to temporally and spatially
localize three stages of a state change (initial, transition-
ing, and end) in instructional videos, using pseudo-labels
from off-the-shelf vision-language systems. SPARTA [23]
demonstrates robotic manipulation capabilities built upon
spatially-progressing state change segmentation. While these
methods produce spatially grounded outputs at the progress
level, they do not maintain consistent object identities across
transformation boundaries. In contrast, our method produces
persistent spatiotemporal tracks that can be queried after the
fact for any object at any time.
Vision and Language. MLLMs such as GPT-4 [1] and Gem-
ini [33] can reason about visual and textual queries [12, 13,
22, 32, 36], but lack pixel-level grounding and produce only
coarse event-level descriptions. In our work, we use VLMs
to verify, refine, and describe the state changes identified
through spatiotemporal reasoning over tubelets.

3.1. Task Formulation
We introduce a prompt-free formulation of Track Any
State [31] that aims to recover all object tracks and iden-
tify their transformations (see Appendix A.1 for an opera-
tive definition of transformations). Formally, given a video

input, and the set of output object tracks and state changes
are restricted to the subset involving the prompt object.
3.2. Preliminaries
We build on the observation in TubeletGraph [31] that the
decomposition of an object results in the appearance of new
tracks. For example, in Figure 1 (top), when the bag is
removed from the box (the prompt object), the tracker [27]
follows the box, leaving the bag untracked. TubeletGraph
initializes a new track for this untracked region and reasons
about its relation to the prompt object to identify the trans-
formation. However, TubeletGraph cannot detect assembly,
as assembly does not trigger the appearance of new tracks
during forward tracking.
Since assembly is decomposition in reverse, tracking
backward allows assembly to be detected through the emer-
gence of new tracks in the reverse direction. We therefore
build spatiotemporal partitions [31] in both the forward and
the backward directions. We then consider tracks that newly
emerge in the middle of the video in either direction, and use
both forward and reverse partitions to reason about whether
these tracks are the signatures of decomposition or assembly
events.
We now describe each step of this pipeline in detail.
3.3. Building Bidirectional Spatiotemporal Parti-
tions
We construct the forward spatiotemporal partition of the
video following TubeletGraph [31]: we segment every frame
with CropFormer [26], propagate every entity in the first
frame forward with SAM2 [27], and at each subsequent
frame, initialize a new track for any entity whose area is cov-
ered by less than τcoverage of existing tracks. During propaga-
tion, multiple tracks may collapse onto the same object (e.g.,
during assembly). To maintain a set of spatially distinct enti-
ties, if two tracks that have each been tracked for more than 5
frames share an IoU greater than τdup, we terminate the track
with lower confidence. We repeat this process in reverse to
obtain the reverse partition (see the supplementary for video
visualizations of both partitions). We represent the forward
and reverse partitions as pools of tubelets, PF = {PF,i} and
PR = {PR,i}, where each tubelet Pi = {ei

Decomposition via Forward Emergence. Suppose a
tubelet PF,i newly emerges at frame t > 1 in the forward
tracking direction, as shown in Figure 2 (left). To determine
whether this tubelet corresponds to a transformation product
or a newly introduced object, we look at all reverse tubelets
at frame t. If PF,i matches a reverse tubelet PR,j, we search
backward in time for up to ω seconds. If, at an earlier frame
t −δ within this window, PR,j matches a previously tracked
tubelet PF,k, we identify a candidate decomposition in which
PF,i originated from PF,k. We mark PF,i and PR,j as dupli-
cates since they correspond to the same post-transformation
object and merge them during final tubelet construction. We
record the state change as (t, {PF,k}, {PF,k, PF,i}).
Assembly via Reverse Emergence. Similarly, suppose
a tubelet PR,i newly emerges at frame t < T in the reverse
tracking direction, as shown in Figure 2 (right). We compare
it with all forward tubelets at frame t. If PR,i matches PF,j,
we search forward in time for up to ω seconds. If, at a
later frame t + δ within this window, PF,j matches PR,k,
we identify a candidate assembly in which PR,i merges
into PR,k. We mark PR,i and PF,j as duplicates since they
correspond to the same pre-transformation object and merge
them during final tubelet construction. We record the state
change as (t, {PR,i, PR,k}, {PR,k}). This yields a set of
candidate transformations.
3.5. Transformation Verification and Realignment
At this point, the candidate transformations have two po-
tential issues. First, our detected state changes may not
represent actual state changes due to coincidental spatial
overlap. Second, the detected frame t may differ from the
frame ttrue at which the transformation actually occurs. In a
decomposition, SAM2 [27] often keeps a single mask over
the separating objects for several frames, so PF,i emerges
at some t > ttrue. In reverse tracking, SAM2 keeps a single
mask over the merged objects past the point where they sepa-
rate in reverse time, so PR,i emerges at some t < ttrue. Thus,
decompositions are often detected late and assemblies early.
We address both issues simultaneously with a VLM. Al-
though a VLM cannot spatially ground transformations, we
leverage it for semantic and temporal reasoning about candi-
date events. For each candidate transformation, we prompt
the VLM with full frames sampled uniformly from the video.
We then provide frames sampled around the predicted event,
each cropped to the union of the participating objects’ bound-
ing boxes with their contours overlaid. The full frames give
context for the whole video, and the crops show the ob-
jects closely enough to verify the transformation and locate
it in time. Based on this observation that decompositions
are predicted late and assembly events are predicted early,
we sample frames asymmetrically. For a suspected decom-
position at t, we sample frames from [t −ωlong, t + ωshort]
where ωshort < ωlong. For a suspected assembly at t, we
sample frames from [t −ωshort, t + ωlong]. We ask the VLM
to determine whether a transformation actually occurred. If
the VLM confirms a transformation, it outputs the frame
at which separation first becomes visually apparent for de-
compositions or merging becomes visually complete for
assembly events. The returned frame index is used as the
final event time (Figure 3). The VLM also names the action
and the objects involved in the pre- and post-transformation
states, completing our state changes S.
3.6. Producing a Unified Spatiotemporal Partition
Once we have detected the state transformations, we pro-
ceed to produce a final, unified set of tubelets that form our
spatiotemporal partition.
We begin by identifying duplicate tubelets across the
forward and reverse partitions PF and PR. For every pair
(PF,i, PR,j) ∈PF × PR, we compute the video-level mean
IoU over their temporal overlap. We then perform Hungarian
matching [17] between forward and reverse tubelets, retain-
ing matched pairs exceeding τmerge, along with the matches
identified during transformation detection. For each matched
pair, we take the union of their timestamps and masks, pro-
ducing a single tubelet. We initialize our final set of tracks
T with these unified tubelets and update the pre- and post-
tracks of each detected transformation to point to this set.
We also add tracks involved in a transformation without a
duplicate in the other direction to T .

Finally, we clean up the tracks T both spatially and tempo-

Metrics. For object tracking on existing benchmarks,
which contain only decomposition transformations, we fol-
low VOST [34] and report Jaccard J over the entire video
and Jtr over the last 25% of frames, computed on the union
of the masks of the prompt object and its resulting frag-
ments. However, distinct objects combine during assembly,
requiring tubelets to be evaluated individually. For object
tracking on OMNIST, we therefore construct a new track-
ing metric analogous to J that evaluates individual tubelets.
Since object identities may be ambiguous during transfor-
mations, we exclude frames within annotated transformation
intervals and split ground-truth tracks into temporally stable
tubelets at transformation boundaries. We similarly split
predicted tracks at their predicted transformation boundaries.
We then apply Hungarian matching [17] between predicted
and ground-truth tubelets using mean IoU over all frames
of the ground-truth tubelet. We report Jtubelet, the average
matched tubelet IoU over objects participating in transforma-
tions, with unmatched ground-truth tubelets assigned zero.
To evaluate transformation detection on VOST-TAS and
OMNIST, we follow the evaluation protocol of Tubelet-
Graph [31], additionally reporting spatiotemporal and overall
precision for OMNIST:
1. Temporal localization precision and recall (TP , TR)
measure whether predicted transformations fall within
ground-truth intervals, extended by 0.5 s on each side. TP
is the fraction of detections correctly localized, while TR
is the fraction of ground-truth transformations correctly
localized.
2. For temporally localized transformations, we report se-
mantic accuracy for predicted action verbs (SV ) and
predicted object descriptions (SO) for objects that are
matched with IoU > 0.5. Verb and object correctness are
judged by GPT-4.1 [1].
3. Spatiotemporal precision and recall (HST,P , HST ) re-
quire both successful temporal localization and participat-
ing objects to be matched with IoU > 0.5 at the extended
annotated boundaries.
4. Overall precision and recall (HP , H) require successful
spatiotemporal localization and correct verb and object
descriptions.
Implementation Details. ATLAS uses the same back-
bone models as TubeletGraph: CropFormer-Hornet3X [26]
for entity segmentation, SAM2.1-L [27] for tracking, and
GPT-4.1 [1] (temperature 0) for state graph construction.
Thresholds are selected once on a separate set of videos; see
Appendix A.5 for analysis of robustness to these choices.
5.2. Baselines
On OMNIST, we compare ATLAS with VLM-based base-
lines and prompt-free adaptations of TubeletGraph [31]:
• GPT-4.1 + GDINO uses GPT-4.1 [1] to predict transfor-
mation events directly from sampled video frames, then
grounds the referenced objects with Grounding DINO [19]
and tracks them with SAM2 [27].
• GPT-4.1 + SAM3 uses the same GPT-4.1 predictions, but
grounds and tracks the referenced objects with SAM3 [4].
• TubeletGraph [31] runs the original TubeletGraph
pipeline initialized with all CropFormer entities [26] in the
first frame.
• FluxGraph [30] is an efficient variant of TubeletGraph.
• TubeletGraph-R runs TubeletGraph on the reversed
video, so that assemblies appear as decompositions and
can be detected.
• TubeletGraph-Bidir runs TubeletGraph independently in
both directions to detect decompositions and assemblies,
unlike ATLAS, which jointly reasons over both partitions
(Figure 2). It then matches forward and reverse tracks via
Hungarian matching [17] and merges matched pairs when

Table 2 compares ATLAS with all baselines on OMNIST. AT-

For transformation detection, ATLAS achieves the highest

Bidir, improving from 16.2 to 36.1, while achieving 4× the
overall precision (HP ), improving from 12.2 to 48.2. A
breakdown by transformation type in Appendix A.4 shows
that ATLAS improves overall recall over TubeletGraph-Bidir
from 9.0 to 34.7 on assembly events and from 22.8 to 37.3
on decomposition events. Even applying our VLM stage to
TubeletGraph-Bidir (+ Verif. & Realign.) closes only part of
this gap, reaching H = 23.2 and HP = 29.3. The remain-
ing gap reflects the contribution of jointly reasoning over
the two partitions, suggesting that running TubeletGraph
independently in both directions is not enough.
To understand where these gains come from, we analyze
the transformations that ATLAS recovers but TubeletGraph-
Bidir misses. First, as discussed, TubeletGraph misses de-
compositions of fragments produced earlier in the video (e.g.,
crackers removed from a bag that was first taken out of its
box). ATLAS recovers 8 such decompositions in OMNIST.
Second, TubeletGraph accepts a new tubelet as a transfor-
mation product only if it satisfies both a spatial proximity
prior and a semantic prior which requires high FC-CLIP [42]
feature similarity between the new tubelet and the prompt
object and often fails for assembly. In 13 transformations
recovered by ATLAS, TubeletGraph’s spatial proximity prior
was satisfied but not its semantic prior (e.g., an SD card
inserted into a camera, a pair of sunglasses inserted into an
eyeglass case, a cleaning cloth removed from an eyeglass
case).
On the other hand, VLM-based baselines achieve the
highest temporal localization and high semantic accuracy,
demonstrating that a VLM can reliably identify what is hap-
pening and when. For temporal localization, VLM-based
baselines outperform ATLAS, which misses some transfor-
mations that do not result in new tubelets. However, GPT-4.1
+ SAM3 yields only about a third of ATLAS’s spatiotem-
poral recall and overall recall (HST = 15.6, H = 11.3),
indicating that it struggles to localize the objects involved in
transformations.

A.1. Defining transformations
A relevant question that readers might have is what we define
as transformations. We frame this definition by looking to
cognitive science. Classical models of object perception in
cognitive science [29] identify objects as being cohesive; i.e.,
the points on them form a connected surface at all times. We
therefore consider transformations as events that break this
property: either by causing sets of points to not be cohesive
anymore (decomposition events) or by causing new sets of
points to begin to move cohesively (assembly events).
A.2. Dataset Details
Data Collection. We collect videos spanning household
activities, cooking, and assembly tasks captured from both
egocentric and third-person viewpoints with no jump cuts.
The resulting dataset includes a broad range of actions such
as removal, insertion, opening, closing, cutting, assembly,
breaking, attachment, unscrewing, capping, pouring, scoop-
ing, peeling, connecting, splitting, and tearing. These actions
are applied across diverse objects ranging from food items
(e.g., oranges, pineapples, broccoli, peppers, eggs, pears)
to household items (e.g., bottles, blenders, jars, sunglasses,
toothbrushes, instruments), electronics (e.g., phones, ear-
buds, remotes, calculators, keyboards), furniture parts (e.g.,
chairs), and toys (e.g., LEGO bricks, train tracks, Play-Doh).
Videos are sourced from: (1) recordings by the authors with
an iPhone; (2) YouTube tutorials retrieved via action-object
queries (e.g., “how to replace Swiffer mop pad”, “how to
mount a car tire”); and (3) existing benchmarks including
VOST [34], VSCOS [41], M3-VOS [5], and IKEA Video
Manuals [21].
Annotation Protocol. We annotate decomposition and
assembly events only when all participating objects can be
clearly identified before and after the transformation, so
that every annotated event has pre- and post-transformation
objects.

tation masks throughout the video. Given the density of
per-frame annotation, we provide sparse manual masks in
our first pass on key frames, and use SAM2 [27] to automat-
ically propagate the masks across the sequence. Then, we
review the masks by SAM2 and correct them where neces-
sary using a polygon-add and polygon-erase tool to finalize
segmentation masks for each object oj. Finally, we annotate
each transformation τi with its temporal boundaries (ts

A.3. Evaluation on Prompt-Based Benchmarks
Incorporating Prompt Masks. VOST [34], VSCOS [41],
M3-VOS [5], and VOST-TAS [31] provide a first-frame
mask for each prompt object. For comparison with prompt-
based methods on these benchmarks, we incorporate the
prompt masks into the initial entity set of the forward parti-
tion. Following TubeletGraph [31], we first resolve overlaps
between the prompt masks and CropFormer [26] entities.
For each CropFormer entity, we compute the fraction of its
area overlapping with the union of prompt masks. We keep
entities with overlap at most τcoverage = 0.25 unchanged,
remove overlapping pixels for overlap between τcoverage and
τremove = 0.75, and discard entities with overlap at least
τremove. We then initialize a separate tubelet for each prompt
mask, retaining its correspondence to the original prompt for
evaluation. The reverse partition is constructed from the last
frame as usual.
Object Tracking Evaluation.
For VOST [34], VS-
COS [41], and M3-VOS [5], we derive the predicted masks
for each prompt object as follows. Initially, the prompt object
is represented by the tubelet initialized from its first-frame
mask. Following a detected decomposition, we track the
resulting objects and output the union of their masks. For
assembly, if the prompt object merges into another object,
its predicted track terminates at the detected transformation.
If another object merges into the prompt object, we con-
tinue tracking the resulting object, whose masks include
both objects.
For M3-VOS, we exclude 20 videos that continue annotat-
ing the inserted object after assembly (e.g., tracking a puzzle
piece separately after it has been inserted into a puzzle),
whereas our formulation updates the larger object’s mask to
include the inserted object and no longer tracks the inserted

Computational Efficiency. The main computational bot-
tleneck of ATLAS is constructing the forward and reverse
spatiotemporal partitions. On OMNIST, entity segmentation
takes 1.25 seconds per frame while tracking takes 2.97 sec-
onds per frame on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU, as
ATLAS propagates all entities jointly in a single SAM2 [27]
pass per direction. TubeletGraph [31] also segments every
frame, but tracking in the forward direction takes 13.04 sec-
onds per frame, as it tracks one prompted entity at a time,
requiring a separate pass for each entity detected by Crop-
Former [26] in the first frame. Tracking in reverse as well,
as in TubeletGraph-Bidir, doubles this tracking cost. Flux-
Graph [30], an efficient variant of TubeletGraph, reduces
entity segmentation to 0.47 seconds per frame, but it also
tracks one entity at a time, totaling 6.45 seconds per frame.
A.6. Limitations
ATLAS detects a transformation only when a tubelet newly
emerges in the forward or reverse direction. Transformations
that do not split or merge objects, such as gradual in-place
state changes (e.g., melting or deformation), produce no
new tubelets and are not detected. Moreover, like Tubelet-
Graph [31], ATLAS relies on the tracker’s false negatives: the
tracker must reliably fail to extend an existing mask over a
transformation product for a new tubelet to emerge. Finally,
constructing both a forward and a reverse spatiotemporal
partition is costly. However, the two partitions are fully inde-
pendent and can be computed in parallel, and improvements
in efficiency [30] directly reduce the cost of both.
