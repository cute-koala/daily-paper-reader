Abstract
Existing UAV tracking benchmarks predominantly rely on
perspective cameras with limited fields of view. Omnidirec-
tional cameras enable full-spherical observation, yet track-
ing from moving UAV platforms remains largely unexplored.
Unlike ground-level panoramic tracking, UAV-mounted omni-
directional cameras observe targets across both azimuth and
elevation, introducing geometric challenges under equirect-
angular projection (ERP). To investigate this setting, we cap-
ture approximately 40 hours of real-world 8K panoramic
UAV video and construct PanoUAV-Track through temporal
clip extraction, target annotation, and the integration of sim-
ulated sequences. The resulting benchmark comprises 1,895
sequences, 507,961 frames, and 2,872 annotated objects,
covering real-world daytime and nighttime scenes alongside
simulation, and supporting both visual and language-based
target specification. Through extensive evaluations, we re-
veal a clear perspective-to-spherical generalization gap
and identify tracking degradation associated with latitude-
dependent distortion and ERP seam discontinuity. To ad-
dress these challenges, we propose PanoUAV-Tracker (PU-
Track), a geometry-aware tracking framework combining
latitude-adaptive search and seam-aware periodic sampling.
PUTrack improves zero-shot and in-domain tracking, par-
ticularly for seam-crossing targets. Code and benchmark
resources will be available1.

tive cameras with limited fields of view[15–17, 40]. Under
such observations, targets can easily disappear from the im-

Omnidirectional cameras offer a promising solution by

spherical field of view. Existing panoramic benchmarks,
such as 360VOT [10], 360VOTS [33], and PanoVOS [37],

directional cameras experience continuously changing aerial
viewpoints, where targets may appear at arbitrary azimuths
and elevations (Figure 1(c)). We refer to this challenging
setting as full-spherical UAV object tracking.
Beyond extending the field of view, full-spherical ob-
servation introduces fundamentally different tracking chal-
lenges. Omnidirectional videos are typically represented
using equirectangular projection (ERP), which maps spheri-
cal observations onto a rectangular image plane. However,
this representation does not preserve the geometric prop-
erties of the sphere. As illustrated in Figure 1(d, e.1–e.3),
target appearance varies with latitude under ERP, while seam-

Consequently, a target that moves continuously on the sphere
may exhibit substantial appearance deformation or abrupt
displacement in the projected image. These effects chal-

about the generalization of perspective-based trackers to
full-spherical observations.
To systematically investigate these challenges, we intro-
duce PanoUAV-Track, a large-scale benchmark for full-
spherical UAV visual tracking. The benchmark comprises
1,895 sequences, 507,961 frames, and 2,872 annotated
objects, covering 951 daytime, 606 nighttime, and 338 simu-
lated sequences. It provides diverse aerial viewpoints, target
trajectories, and observation conditions, together with vi-
sual and language-based target specifications. Based on
this benchmark, we investigate three fundamental research
questions: (RQ1) How well do perspective trackers gen-
eralize to full-spherical UAV observations? (RQ2) Does
the generalization gap persist across different observation
domains? (RQ3) How does spherical projection geometry
affect tracking performance? Our comprehensive evalua-
tions reveal a substantial perspective-to-spherical generaliza-
tion gap, with tracking performance particularly affected by
latitude-dependent distortion and ERP seam discontinuities.
Motivated by these findings,
we further propose
PanoUAV-Tracker (PUTrack), a geometry-aware track-
ing framework specifically designed for full-spherical UAV
observations. PUTrack combines template-guided target
localization with geometry-aware search construction. Its
Geometry-Aware Search Adapter (GASA) incorporates
Latitude-Adaptive Patch Sampling (LAPS) to compensate
for latitude-dependent distortion and Seam-Aware Search
Cropping (SASC) to preserve spatial continuity across
panoramic boundaries. Thus, PUTrack maintains coherent
target localization in ERP coordinates.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Full-spherical UAV tracking. We investigate visual track-
ing using UAV-mounted omnidirectional cameras, extend-
ing aerial tracking to full-spherical observations.
• PanoUAV-Track benchmark and analysis. PanoUAV-
Track supports visual and language-based tracking. Ex-
tensive evaluations reveal the limitations of perspective
trackers under full-spherical observations.
• PanoUAV-Tracker (PUTrack). A geometry-aware track-
ing framework is developed with latitude-adaptive search
and periodic seam-aware sampling.

3.2. The PanoUAV-Track Benchmark
Data Collection and Processing. As illustrated in Fig-
ure 2, the construction of PanoUAV-Track follows a three-
stage pipeline.
We collect approximately 40 hours of
real-world 8K omnidirectional videos using UAV-mounted
panoramic cameras, supplemented with simulated aerial
sequences. All videos are processed and converted into
360◦× 180◦equirectangular projection (ERP) format. The
resulting recordings are further segmented into tracking clips
based on scene continuity and target visibility.
Trajectory Annotation and Quality Control. We adopt
a semi-automatic annotation pipeline combining manual ini-
tialization, automatic tracking, and human verification. For
each sequence, annotators first select the target and man-
ually specify its initial bounding box. An existing tracker
then propagates the target state across subsequent frames,
followed by a filtering step to refine the initial trajectory. To
ensure annotation quality, all generated trajectories undergo
frame-level manual inspection and correction, particularly
addressing inaccurate bounding-box sizes and missing anno-
tations. Additional attention is paid to projection distortions
and targets crossing the ERP seam. Automatic tracking
serves only as an annotation aid, while the final ground-truth
trajectories are determined through human verification and
correction.
Referring Expression Annotation. To support language-
guided tracking, we further associate each target trajectory
with a natural-language referring expression. Candidate
descriptions are first generated with the assistance of Qwen3
[38] based on the target and its surrounding visual context.
These descriptions are subsequently reviewed and manually
corrected at the sequence level to ensure target specificity
and reduce ambiguity.
The resulting benchmark provides aligned ERP video
frames, frame-level bounding-box annotations, and language
prompts. The same ground-truth trajectories are shared
across visual-prompt, language-prompt, and joint visual–
language tracking protocols, enabling consistent evaluation
under different target-specification settings.

4. PanoUAV-Tracker
We propose PanoUAV-Tracker (PUTrack), a geometry-
aware visual tracking framework for full-spherical UAV ob-
servations. Unlike conventional perspective tracking, ERP
introduces latitude-dependent geometric distortion and hor-
izontal boundary discontinuities, leading to inconsistent
search coverage, localization drift, and fragmented target
observations, as shown in Figure 1. PUTrack addresses these
challenges through a unified tracking pipeline that combines
template-guided target localization, geometry-aware search
construction, and consistent coordinate recovery. The overall
framework is illustrated in Figure 4.

5.5. Perspective-to-Spherical Analysis
RQ1. How Well Do Perspective Trackers Generalize to
Full-Spherical UAV Observation? We evaluate generaliza-
tion using matched ERP and perspective observations, defin-
ing the gap as Δgeo = AUCERP −AUCPER. Table 5 shows
that all six baselines degrade under ERP observations. PU-
Track also exhibits negative gaps but substantially reduces
daytime and simulation degradation, indicating improved
generalization to full-spherical UAV tracking.
RQ2. Does the Generalization Gap Persist across Day-
time, Nighttime, and Simulated Domains? Table 5 reveals
gaps across all three domains. Baseline AUC degradation
ranges from 5.90–7.60 points during daytime, 3.85–15.84
at night, and 19.40–20.54 in simulation. PUTrack reduces
the corresponding gaps to 0.85, 8.28, and 2.75 points. It
also achieves the highest daytime and simulation ERP AUC,
exceeding the best baselines by 4.99 and 15.08 points, respec-
tively. These results show daytime and simulation improve-
ments, while nighttime generalization remains challenging.
RQ3. Is Tracking Degradation Systematically Associated
with Spherical Projection Geometry? Table 6 analyzes per-
formance across latitude regions and ERP seam crossings.
Baseline AUC increases by 5.46 points on average from
Equator to Middle, then decreases by 4.02 points toward
Polar. PUTrack achieves 84.75 overall AUC and reduces the
Equator-to-Middle variation to 2.38 points, although its Po-
lar AUC remains 5.43 points below Middle. It also reduces
the seam drop from the baseline average of 33.17 points to
1.19 points, achieving 83.43 AUC within seam-event win-
dows. These findings indicate that PUTrack substantially
alleviates ERP seam discontinuity, while latitude-dependent
distortion remains challenging.
6. Conclusion
We introduce PanoUAV-Track, a large-scale benchmark for
full-spherical UAV tracking across daytime, nighttime, and
simulated scenarios. Extensive evaluations reveal a substan-
tial perspective-to-spherical generalization gap, highlight-
ing challenges from latitude-dependent distortion and ERP
seam crossings. To address this gap, we propose PanoUAV-
Tracker (PUTrack), a geometry-aware tracking framework
incorporating the Geometry-Aware Search Adapter (GASA).
By combining latitude-adaptive search and seam-aware sam-
pling, PUTrack achieves robust full-spherical tracking with-
out modifying the underlying network. Experiments demon-
strate consistent improvements under both zero-shot and
in-domain settings, particularly for seam-crossing targets.

A. Cross-Dataset Generalization
To assess cross-dataset generalization, we evaluate OSTrack
and PUTrack on 360Tracking, comprising 21 sequences and
9,909 annotated frames. Both methods share identical check-
points and initialization conditions within each comparison,
without dataset-specific training or hyperparameter tuning.
Following our circular-IoU protocol, evaluation covers 9,725
valid visible frames, including 383 seam frames from 13 se-
quences. As shown in Table 7, PUTrack improves AUC and
AUCS from 54.41% and 32.31% to 72.23% and 67.18%, re-
spectively, using the released weights. With weights trained
on our dataset, PUTrack achieves consistent improvements
of 11.18 and 29.31 percentage points in AUC and AUCS,
respectively. These results support the transferability of the
proposed seam-aware search mechanism, particularly un-
der seam-crossing conditions. However, the lower absolute
performance of the checkpoints trained on our dataset and
the limited benchmark scale warrant caution when drawing
broader conclusions about cross-dataset generalization.
B. Data Audit
B.1. Data Acquisition and Scene-Disjoint Split
The real-world videos were independently collected using
Antigravity A1 and DJI Avata 360. All videos were exported
at 30 fps. The original ERP frames were recorded at 8K
resolution and subsequently resized to 1920 × 960 for an-
notation and evaluation. Each recording session captured
a different real-world scene. We therefore identify scenes
using their recording timestamps and perform dataset
splitting at the scene level. All clips and target trajectories
collected at the same recording time are assigned to the same
subset, ensuring that the training, validation, and test scenes
are mutually disjoint.

D.1. Target-Centric Perspective Evaluation
We compare tracking performance on original ERP se-
quences and target-centric perspective sequences generated
from the same videos. Each pair preserves the target identity
and source-frame correspondence, allowing performance to
be examined under these two observation settings.
Perspective sequence generation and annotation.
For
each source-visible frame, the virtual camera is oriented to-
ward the target center while retaining temporal lag in camera
orientation and residual target motion. A rectilinear view
with horizontal and vertical fields of view of 90◦is rendered
at 480 × 480 resolution by inverse ray mapping and bilinear
interpolation. ERP sampling wraps horizontally and clamps
vertical coordinates to the valid image range. Ground-truth
bounding boxes are then annotated directly in the rendered
perspective images; they are not obtained by projecting the
ERP bounding boxes. Source annotations are used only to
construct the offline perspective sequences. During tracking,
each method receives the first-frame box annotated in its
respective representation and the language or category input
required by its protocol. No subsequent ground-truth boxes
or virtual-camera parameters are supplied to the tracker.
Fairness of Perspective-to-Spherical Evaluation.
Our
evaluation focuses on visual target tracking rather than ac-
tive viewpoint planning. Therefore, using ground-truth target
locations to guide virtual camera orientation during offline
perspective sequence generation is a reasonable data con-
struction strategy. This process primarily ensures target
visibility within the limited perspective field of view while
retaining temporal lag in camera orientation and residual
target motion. Importantly, ground-truth information is used
exclusively for offline data generation and is not provided
to trackers during inference. All evaluated trackers receive
identical perspective sequences under the same evaluation
protocol, ensuring fairness across methods. The perspective
𝐴𝑈𝐶𝑃𝐸𝑅serves as a reference for quantifying performance
degradation when transferring from conventional perspective
observations to full-spherical ERP observations, not as an
independent benchmark for active camera tracking. Con-
sequently, this comparison characterizes the generalization
gap between the two observation settings while maintaining
consistent evaluation conditions across trackers.
Matched evaluation.
For each paired trajectory, evalua-
tion uses the intersection of source-frame indices with valid
annotations in both representations, excluding initialization
and invisible-target frames. Perspective predictions are eval-
uated against the newly annotated perspective boxes using
planar IoU, while ERP predictions are evaluated using cir-
cular IoU. Both metrics use the same intersection-over-
union definition; circular IoU accounts for horizontal
periodicity when computing intersection and union areas.
Their scores are therefore comparable under the same
overlap-based scoring protocol. Success AUC is computed
using trapezoidal integration over 21 thresholds from 0 to 1
and averaged equally across valid targets.
Interpretation.
We report the performance difference
Δgeo = 𝐴𝑈𝐶ERP −𝐴𝑈𝐶PER,
(29)
where negative values indicate higher AUC on the target-
centric perspective sequences. This comparison measures
performance under two specified observation settings. Target
centering is an explicit property of the perspective dataset
construction. It changes apparent motion, target position,
field of view, and image sampling. Moreover, IoU-based
evaluation in each representation is not generally projection-
invariant. Accordingly, the difference characterizes these
paired settings and is not attributed solely to ERP projection
distortion.
E. Search-Cropping Strategies with LAPS
This section specifies the seven search-cropping strategies
(S0–S6) compared in Table 4c.
Shared setup.
All variants use the same released OSTrack
checkpoint, first-frame initialization, and constant-padded
ERP template (128 × 128, template factor 2), without addi-
tional training. The search factor is 4, and the search input is
resized to 256 × 256. Each variant applies the same LAPS
rule (𝜖= 0.20, 𝛼max = 1.25) to its previous prediction to
determine the search center and extent. The variants run
independently, so their predicted states may diverge over
time. S0–S5 use one network forward per frame; S6 uses
one or two. For the planar crops in S0–S4 and S6, vertical
overflow is zero padded.
S0: Constant padding.
The LAPS-expanded search rect-
angle is cropped directly from the ERP image. Samples
outside the image are filled with zeros, and the resulting
patch is resized using bilinear interpolation. S0 therefore
serves as the LAPS-enabled planar-padding control; it does
not connect content across the horizontal ERP seam.
S1: Border replication.
Horizontal out-of-bounds sam-
ples are replaced by the nearest left or right boundary pixel,
followed by bilinear resizing. This extends the boundary
appearance into the missing region instead of introducing
zeros, but does not recover scene content from the opposite
ERP boundary.
4


S2: Reflection padding.
Horizontal out-of-bounds sam-
ples are obtained by reflecting image content at the corre-
sponding boundary using BORDER_REFLECT_101, which
does not repeat the boundary pixel. The patch is then resized
bilinearly. Unlike periodic sampling, reflection creates mir-
rored local content.
S3: Periodic nearest.
When the search rectangle crosses
the horizontal seam, integer source columns are wrapped
modulo the ERP width 𝑊and gathered into a contiguous
patch. Otherwise, the original constant-padded crop is used.
The entire patch is resized using nearest-neighbor interpo-
lation on every frame. Thus, S3 changes the resizing policy
globally, not only at seam pixels.
S4: Periodic bilinear.
S4 uses the same horizontal wrap-
ping and patch assembly as S3, but applies bilinear resizing.
For rectangles that do not cross the seam, it retains the orig-
inal constant-padded bilinear crop. This strategy joins the
actual scene content on both sides of the ERP seam within a
single search input. It is the SASC configuration used in the
selected GASA variant.
S5:
BFoV/eBFoV reprojection.
S5 uses the official
360VOT geometry implementation [10, 33] to construct a
local view centered on the previous prediction, with zero
roll. For an ERP image of size 𝑊× 𝐻and LAPS-expanded
search dimensions (𝑤𝑐, ℎ𝑐), the angular extents are
𝜃ℎ= min(360◦, 360◦𝑤𝑐/𝑊),
𝜃𝑣= min(180◦, 180◦ℎ𝑐/𝐻).
(30)
The released automatic rule selects a tangent view when
both extents are at most 90◦, and a spherical view other-
wise. The view is sampled at 256 × 256 using the library’s
nearest-neighbor remapping and boundary conventions. The
predicted local box is clipped to the local image, inverse-
projected through the stored sampling map, and converted
to a circular ERP box for the next state update.
The template remains in ERP, and the search extent fol-
lows LAPS. S5 evaluates this reprojection operator within
the host tracker, not the complete 360VOT/360VOTS tem-
poral framework: it does not include their separate angular
search policy, loss-state holding, progressive expansion, or
full-frame recovery.
S6: Dual-view search.
S6 first evaluates the constant-
padded view. If the predicted search rectangle crosses the
seam, it additionally evaluates a periodic-bilinear view, pro-
vided that this patch contains valid image content and differs
from the first patch. Both views use the same pre-update
state, template, and model weights.
Each candidate is considered supported when at least 50%
of its predicted box is covered by valid image pixels; padding
and out-of-view area count as unsupported. If only one
candidate is supported, it is selected. If both are supported,
selection uses the peak-to-sidelobe ratio (PSR) of their Hann-
windowed response maps, excluding a 5 × 5 neighborhood
around each peak. The periodic candidate is selected only
when its PSR is higher. Ties and cases with two unsupported
candidates retain the constant-padded candidate. The tracker
updates its state once from the selected prediction, without
averaging candidates or using ground truth.
F0–F1: Complete framework adapters.
F0 (360VOT)
and F1 (360VOTS) use exactly the same released OSTrack
ViT-B/16 epoch-300 checkpoint as GASA, verified by SHA-
256, without fine-tuning. Both project the template and
search region to local BFoV/eBFoV views and inverse-
project predictions to update the spherical state. F0 fixes the
template/search factors to 2/4 without validation-set tuning
and accepts every finite prediction. F1 fixes SR Ratio = 2
and Max Loss = 4, and selects SR Min = 5◦and confidence
threshold 𝜏= 0.25 from {5◦, 90◦} × {0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75} us-
ing target-averaged AUC on all 277 validation targets; these
settings are frozen before testing. Rejected F1 predictions
count as failures and trigger search-region holding, progres-
sive expansion, and ultimately full-ERP recovery. Projected
inputs may differ in appearance and target scale from OS-
Track’s perspective training crops, but no controlled ablation
isolates this effect. Since F0 and F1 share the same template
projection, their performance gap cannot be attributed to
that factor alone; their search and validity/recovery policies
also differ. Projection-related domain mismatch therefore
remains a possible, unverified contributor to F1’s lower per-
formance.

G.5. GASA Integration
MCITrack, ODTrack, ORTrack, and DUTrack with
GASA. We integrate GASA into the search-image branch of
MCITrack, ODTrack, ORTrack, and DUTrack while preserv-
ing their original tracking networks and model-specific com-
ponents. The proposed adapter combines latitude-adaptive
horizontal search expansion with periodic seam-aware sam-
pling. Predictions are mapped back into ERP coordinates
using geometry-consistent coordinate recovery. For zero-
shot evaluation, GASA is directly applied to the released
pretrained models of MCITrack, ODTrack, ORTrack, and
DUTrack. For in-domain evaluation, GASA is applied to
the corresponding PanoUAV-Track-trained ORTrack and
DUTrack models without additional optimization. Each
baseline–GASA pair uses the same checkpoint. Both config-
urations preserve a single tracking-network forward pass per
frame.
Effectiveness across trackers. Table 11 shows consistent
improvements across all four trackers. Under zero-shot
evaluation, GASA improves the overall AUC of MCITrack,
ODTrack, ORTrack, and DUTrack by 9.04, 9.10, 8.74, and
9.36 percentage points, respectively. The gains also persist
under in-domain evaluation: GASA improves ORTrack from
74.06 to 82.31 AUC and DUTrack from 76.61 to 82.84 AUC.
Since each baseline–GASA pair uses the same checkpoint,
these improvements demonstrate the benefit of the inference-
time adapter without additional model optimization.
Seam robustness. The largest improvements occur within
seam-event windows. In the zero-shot setting, GASA in-
creases Seam AUC by 48.84, 47.25, 46.07, and 47.44 per-
centage points for MCITrack, ODTrack, ORTrack, and DU-
Track, respectively. Meanwhile, the absolute gap between
Seam and Non-Seam AUC decreases from 38.03–41.45 per-
centage points to at most 1.48 percentage points. A similar
trend holds for the in-domain models: Seam AUC improves
by 43.59 percentage points for ORTrack and 42.42 percent-
age points for DUTrack, while their seam deficits decrease
from 38.99 and 41.83 to 1.40 and 3.18 percentage points,
respectively. These results are consistent with improved
robustness to ERP seam discontinuities.
Performance across latitudes. GASA improves AUC in
all three latitude groups for every evaluated tracker in both
settings. For the zero-shot models, the gains range from
9.80 to 11.61 percentage points in Equator, 6.68 to 7.30
in Middle, and 7.04 to 8.57 in Polar. The Middle–Equator
gap also decreases for all evaluated models in both settings.
However, the gains are not confined to high latitudes, and
performance differences across latitude groups remain. The
results therefore support broad improvements across latitude
conditions.

and SASC. LAPS expands horizontal search coverage ac-
