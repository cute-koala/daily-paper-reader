Abstract
Tracking a fast-moving point in 3D is prohibitive when RGB
frames are blurred or too far apart to reveal its motion.
Event cameras record brightness changes between frames,
providing measurements inside these temporal gaps. We in-
troduce TrackAnyMotion, which combines monocular RGB
frames and events to track arbitrary points in 2D and 3D,
including at times when no RGB frame is available. The
motion expected for a static point, given scene geometry
and camera motion, provides a reference for identifying in-
dependently moving objects.
An independent-motion de-
coder combines geometric motion cues with event guidance

1. Introduction
Tracking any point in 3D is fundamental for a vast va-
riety of tasks spanning from robot imitation and policy
learning [47, 55], dynamic reconstruction [23, 51], motion-
controlled video generation [10, 12], and physical infer-
ence [3, 21, 57].
Despite the recent success of 3D point trackers, these
1

methods are fundamentally limited by the hardware or stan-
dard cameras. A point on a fast-moving object travels far
in the blind-time between consecutive frames, inevitably
motion-blurring and corrupting its own observations. More-
over, standard cameras notably suffer from a bandwidth-
latency trade-off that limits their performance in such cases.
At low bandwidth and low frame rates, fast-objects appear
motion blurred, while at high frame rates, motion blur is
minimized at the expense of high bandwidth.
Event cameras have been shown to be an ideal com-
plement to standard cameras in addressing the bandwidth-
latency trade-off [7].
They are bio-inspired sensors that
asynchronously measure per-pixel brightness changes with
microsecond temporal resolution.
Due to their working
principle, they trigger events sparsely, often in correspon-
dence of moving edges yielding minimal bandwidth and
providing information between subsequent frames.
Recent frame–event trackers such as TAPFormer [29]
exploit event-based motion, demonstrating high-frequency
2D tracking between frames. Yet until now, there exists no
event-based 3D point tracker that leverages a set of monoc-
ular observations of events and frames to estimate the dis-
placement of each point in a scene with high temporal reso-
lution. Moreover, existing methods typically resort to track-
ing a sparse set of points using complex hardware setups
involving multiple cameras
To overcome existing methods, we propose the first 3D
track-any-point method, which leverages the high temporal
resolution of event cameras in combination with standard
frames to estimate 2D and 3D point displacement at high
frequency.
We use the expected motion for a static point as the refer-
ence for tracking and disentangling motion caused by inde-
pendently moving objects and by the static background. By
fusing frames and event features and iteratively condition-
ing 2D and 3D updaters with event features, we are able to
extrapolate the geometry of the scene as well as the 2D-3D
point displacement with high frequency.
With this work, we introduce HyperEvent , a large scale
simulated dataset consisting of high frequency ground truth
of depth, 2D-3D tracks, poses, and interdependently mov-
ing objects. with events and frames. To the best of our
knowledge, this is the first dataset tailored for 3D track-
ing and independently moving object segmentation using
events and frames.
Our tracker outperforms state-of-the-art baselines in both
2D and 3D tracking by 70% and 32%, respectively. More-
over, TrackAnyMotion enables scenarios that are inaccessi-
ble to standard frame-based cameras by tracking points at
arbitrary timestamps in time, outperforming interpolation-
based baselines by 2x in both 2D and 3D.

2. Related work
Point tracking and dynamic 3D reconstruction.
Image-
space trackers such as CoTracker3 [22] learn correspon-
dences from synthetic supervision and pseudo-labelled real
videos, and a growing family of methods lifts these cor-
respondences into 3D. SpatialTracker [58], DELTA [37],
TAPIP3D [61] and TrackingWorld [31] rely on depth
estimated by a separate network, whereas SpatialTrack-
erV2 [59], St4RTrack [6], D4RT [62] and V-DPM [44]
predict scene geometry and point motion jointly in a sin-
gle feed-forward pass. Trace Anything [30] represents ev-
ery trajectory as a B-spline in time, so that positions can
be read off at any instant, and TrackCraft3R [36] and Re-
Lift [42] start from the output of a reconstruction model
and refine tracks on top of it. TrackEverything [20] extends
dense tracking to videos of more than a thousand frames
with a persistent world-space representation.
Our track-
ing backbone is Track4World [32], which predicts world-
centric dense 2D and 3D flow and refines it with iterative
updates in the style of RAFT [45]. All of these methods
see only RGB frames and report positions at frame times.
TrackAnyMotion adds the event stream of the same camera
and predicts 2D and 3D positions in between frames.
Tracking with events and frames.
Event cameras report
per-pixel brightness changes asynchronously and with mi-
crosecond latency, so they observe how the scene moves
while a frame camera is blind between exposures [7]. Early
data-driven trackers followed frame-initialised features with
events alone [34], and ETAP [14] and MATE [15] extended
this to tracking any point from an event stream. Combining
both modalities, FE-TAP [28], TAPFormer [29] and Blink-
Track [41] fuse frames and events to output 2D tracks well
above the frame rate, and our event encoder follows the de-
sign of TAPFormer. Continuous-time formulations go one
step further and model each pixel’s trajectory as a paramet-
ric curve that can be sampled at any time [9, 48], but the tra-
jectories remain in the image plane. In 3D, EMoTive [49]
and Han et al. [16] recover optical flow together with motion
in depth from events, which yields a 3D motion field over
2


a short horizon rather than persistent correspondences. The
event-based trackers that do produce 3D trajectories need
additional sensing: stereo events in E-3DTrack [25], a rig
of four RGB and two event cameras in MER-Tracker [2], or
a depth map as input in E-TraMamba [40]. We work with a
single RGB camera and its co-located events, take no depth
as input, and output long-range 3D tracks of arbitrary points
at timestamps between frames.
Visual geometry models with event information.
Feed-
forward geometry models such as VGGT [50], π3 [52]
and Depth Anything 3 [27] predict depth, pointmaps and
camera parameters directly from an arbitrary set of views,
MonST3R [63] adapts this recipe to scenes with moving
objects, and Flow3r [5] shows that correspondence supervi-
sion can drive geometry and pose learning. Events have
recently entered this family from two directions.
Depth
AnyEvent [1] and EventVGGT [39] distill RGB depth
and geometry models into event-based estimators, while
EAG3R [56] and Weerakoon et al. [54] add an event branch
to pointmap reconstruction for dynamic scenes and diffi-
cult lighting, and Interp3R [13] uses events to interpolate
pointmaps and camera poses to timestamps where no frame
exists. These models produce geometry at each timestamp
but do not link material points across time. We rely on them
for exactly that geometry: Depth Anything 3 provides the
depth and camera poses that serve as the static-scene ref-
erence for our tracking and for the rigid-flow computation
described below.
Independently moving objects segmentation.
Separat-
ing the motion a camera induces from the motion of objects
is a long-standing problem, and the most common tool is a
comparison between the observed motion and the rigid flow
that camera motion alone would produce given the scene
depth. GeoNet [60] introduced this decomposition for un-
supervised depth and pose learning, treating the residual
as object motion. Event-based methods adopted the same
idea in several forms: through motion compensation and
contrast maximization [43], by learning depth, ego-motion
and motion masks jointly [35], or, in Un-EVIMO [53],
by thresholding the rigid-flow residual into pseudo-labels
for an event segmenter. More recent learned segmenters
feed ego-motion-compensated events and temporal atten-
tion to the network [11], predict masks together with future
motion to suppress events on moving objects [38], or su-
pervise boundary ownership alongside motion masks [18].
On the RGB side, DynOSeg [17] fuses a geometry-aware
residual map with depth features, and Segment Any Mo-
tion [19] reads moving objects off long-range tracks. Track-
ers themselves have started to carry motion structure: Spa-
tialTracker [58] and MoSE3 [4] learn rigidity embeddings
for trajectories, and TrackEverything [20] classifies points
as static or dynamic and refines only the dynamic ones.
What these approaches share is that the residual, or the
static-versus-dynamic decision, is computed once, as pre-
processing, as pseudo-labels, or as a single routing step. In
TrackAnyMotion the residual is a running quantity instead:
we recompute it from the current depth, pose and track es-
timates inside every refinement iteration, and use it both to
segment independently moving objects (IMOs) and to de-
cide how much each update should trust the event evidence.
3. Method
Our method jointly estimates 2D and 3D point trajecto-
ries and independent-motion masks from monocular RGB
frames and synchronized events. Our central idea is to use
camera-induced motion as a geometric reference within it-
erative tracking: deviations from this reference help iden-
tify independently moving points, whose learned motion
features in turn condition trajectory refinement. For times-
tamps without an RGB observation, event-conditioned pose
and motion predictions supply the target geometry needed
by the tracker. Fig. 2 summarizes these two mechanisms.

4. Experiments
We evaluate TrackAnyMotion, trained solely on Hyper-
Event, on high-frequency trajectory interpolation, 2D and
3D point tracking, and independent-motion segmentation.
Tests on synthetic and real-world datasets and ablations ex-
amine event input, motion feedback, and tracking at and
between RGB timestamps.
Implementation details.
Our model builds on pretrained
Track4World tracking components and DA3 geometry. At
RGB timestamps, the pairwise inference protocol pro-
cesses each source–target pair independently using two
RGB frames and four refinement iterations in half precision.
Datasets.
HyperEvent contains 1,499 scenes, split into
1,199 training, 150 validation, and 150 test scenes. Across
all 25,621 foreground objects, the average relative speed is
1.458 m/s and the peak relative speed is 63.650 m/s. For
the 3,029 dynamic objects, the average absolute speed is
6.324 m/s and the peak absolute speed is 29.731 m/s. The
native evaluation uses 256×256 RGB images and a 32×32
material-point grid, with RGB observations at 50 Hz and
geometric trajectories at 200 Hz. For external tracking eval-
uation, we use 200 KITTI 2015 image pairs and the released
PointOdyssey and ADT mini subsets. PointOdyssey im-
ages are resized to 640 × 360, while ADT uses its native
512 × 512 resolution. PointOdyssey and ADT are evalu-
ated using independent frame-0-to-target pairs within 16-
and 50-frame prefixes. Events are simulated for Kitti, ADT
and PointOdissey datasets, while DSEC reports real events.
4.1. High-frequency trajectory interpolation
We predict 200 Hz trajectories from 25 Hz RGB frames and
intervening events, including seven intermediate positions
per 40 ms interval. Table 1 compares RGB timestamps (S)
and intermediate timestamps (K) against linear interpola-
tion (LI) of Track4World and TrackEverything tracks, and
TAPFormer [29] for 2D tracking.
At intermediate timestamps, TrackAnyMotion achieves
0.132 px EPE2D, compared with 1.470 px for Track4World
+ LI and 0.497 px for TAPFormer. Its EPE3D is 0.148 m,
versus 0.284 m and 0.382 m for Track4World + LI and
TrackEverything + LI, respectively. Gains also extend to
RGB timestamps and both IMO and static regions. These
cross-model comparisons do not isolate the contributions of
events or motion grounding; matched controls are discussed
in the ablation study. The supplementary section provides
the protocol and detailed results.
4.2. 2D point tracking
We measure 2D tracking accuracy using endpoint error in
input-image pixels and the percentages of predictions with
errors strictly below 1 and 3 pixels. Point coordinates are
converted to input-image pixels before scoring. For our syn-
thetic frame-time evaluation, eligible correspondences have
valid, visible, in-frame source and target observations, fi-
nite ground-truth coordinates, and positive depth. Errors
and accuracy counts are pooled over eligible correspon-
dence occurrences.

4.5. Ablation Study
We investigate tracking under sparse RGB observations us-
ing three variants: T4W–TAPFormer (A), which combines
Track4World with TAPFormer event encoding and fusion
without IMO; Ours (B), which additionally introduces IMO
prediction and conditioning; and T4W–ETAP (C), which
uses ETAP encoding and its associated fusion without IMO.
We also evaluate Ours–ZeroEvents (D), where we zeros
event representation starting from B model while retaining
its weights, fusion connections, IMO branch, timestamps,
and temporal query axis.
Setup and metrics.
We evaluate three held-out scenes at
5 Hz RGB and 200 Hz trajectory sampling, using two RGB
frames 200 ms apart and 41 query timestamps. We report
EPE2D (px) and aligned camera-residual EPE3D (m). De-
tails about sampling, alignment, eligibility, motion group-
ing, and timing are available in the supplementary material.
Different Motion Levels.
We compare static points with
the medium- and high-motion GT-speed groups. Table 5(a)
reports RGB-endpoint tracking errors. The largest event-
input effect occurs in the high-motion group: zeroing events
increases EPE2D from 7.629 to 15.435 px, a 102.31% in-
crease. The effect is not uniform across groups: the zero-
event control has lower static and medium-motion EPE2D.
These results indicate that event input primarily reduces
large 2D tracking errors associated with high speed motion.
Accuracy Changes Between RGB Timestamps.
Ta-
ble 5(b) compares direct predictions with linear interpo-
lation (LI) of each model’s own RGB endpoints at 25%,
50%, and 75% of the inter-frame interval. Our endpoints
are event-conditioned; endpoint and intermediate evalua-
tions use different GT-valid cohorts.
At the three reported phases, our method has lower na-
tive EPE2D than its zero-event control, but higher aligned
residual EPE3D. Interpolation of our own endpoints outper-
forms our direct predictions at all three reported phases for
both errors. Across all 39 phases, its interpolated predic-
tions have lower EPE2D in 37 phases and lower EPE3D in
38 phases. Thus, the sparse-RGB results support the advan-
tage of using event for 2D tracking.
C has no native learned intermediate output in the evalu-

Effectiveness of Components.
Table 5(c) summarizes re-

IMO modeling: B versus A. B adds an IMO decoder with
feedback into 2D and 3D refinement, using the architecture
and supervision described in Secs. 3.4 and 3.5. B has lower
overall tracking errors than A in this pilot.
Event encoding and fusion: C versus A. C uses the ETAP
encoder and gated residual adapters for dense 2D, 3D, and

Event input: D versus B. Zeroing events increases overall

pixel (0.6028 to 0.7021). Events therefore primarily reduce
large 2D errors under high motion, rather than uniformly
improving tracking.
5. Conclusion and Limitations
We presented TrackAnyMotion, a monocular RGB–event
tracker that recovers 2D and 3D point trajectories between
RGB frames.
Its independent-motion feedback connects
event measurements with scene geometry to guide itera-
8


tive trajectory refinement. We also introduced HyperEvent,
providing synchronized frames, events, high-frequency tra-
jectories, geometry, and independent-motion labels.
To-
gether, these contributions advance point tracking beyond
RGB sampling rates. Reliance on monocular geometry and
limited validation with real events remain limitations.

B. High-frequency trajectory interpolation
Protocol and baselines.
RGB observations constrain
track endpoints but do not specify the motion between them.
We evaluate trajectories at 200 Hz from RGB observations
at 25 Hz and intervening events, predicting seven interme-
diate positions per 40 ms interval without intermediate im-
ages. Native geometric trajectories provide the evaluation
targets. We compare our predictions with linear interpola-
tion of RGB-only tracks from Track4World and TrackEv-
erything, and with TAPFormer for 2D tracking. The high-
frequency tracking comparison in the main paper separates
RGB observation timestamps (S axis) from strictly brack-
eted intermediate timestamps (K axis), with 560,615 and
153,519 correspondence occurrences in the 2D evaluation,
respectively. Separate blocks report 2D endpoint error in
pixels and 3D endpoint error in metres, strict and relaxed
accuracies, and regional EPEs for independently moving
objects and static regions. The 2D accuracy thresholds are
strictly below 1 and 3 pixels; 3D AccS/AccR use absolute-
or-relative thresholds of 0.05 and 0.10. For an intermediate

At intermediate timestamps (K

D. Datasets
D.1. HyperEvent
Synthetic Data Generation Pipeline
We generate synthetic training data using a four-stage
pipeline built on the Kubric simulator with MOVi-F-style
scenes: (1) physics simulation with similarity time com-
pression, (2) synchronized dual-rate rendering, (3) event
simulation, and (4) analytical generation of sparse and
dense tracking ground truth.
(1) Scene simulation and similarity time compression.
Each scene is simulated using PyBullet rigid-body physics.
Objects first settle under normal gravity. We then apply sim-
ilarity time compression with a factor of k = 4: linear and
angular velocities are scaled by k, gravity by k2, and the
simulation and rendering rates by k. The resulting trajecto-
ries satisfy x′(t) = x(kt), meaning that the scene follows
the same trajectory but evolves k times faster. This pro-
duces the fast motion that motivates the use of event cam-
eras, while preserving per-frame pixel displacement, frame
count, and rendering cost.
(2) Dual-rate rendering.
We render two synchronized
branches from the same physics simulation, ensuring tem-
poral correspondence between the event and frame data:
• High-rate (HR) branch. Frames are rendered at 250k =
1000 Hz and 256×256 resolution using a GPU rasterizer,
with motion blur disabled. These frames are used as input
to the event simulator.
• Low-rate (LR) branch. We render 50 RGB frames at
30 fps and apply motion blur in post-processing. We also
export aligned depth maps as uint16 PNG images, with
depth in meters decoded as d = v/65535 × 100. These
outputs are used as inputs to RGB baselines and for eval-
uation.
(3) Event simulation.
We generate events from the HR
frames using the CUDA backend of the Event Simulator
(ESIM). Each frame is converted to log-luminance, L =
log(0.299R+0.587G+0.114B). Between consecutive HR
frames, log-luminance is assumed to vary linearly. An event
is emitted when the interpolated log-luminance crosses a
contrast threshold of ±0.2 relative to the per-pixel refer-
ence. Event timestamps are obtained by linear interpolation
and stored in microseconds. The resulting events are sorted
by timestamp.
(4) Ground-truth tracks and correspondences.
We
generate all labels analytically from the simulator state, in-
cluding rigid-body poses and mesh geometry:
• Sparse tracks. We sample 384 points per scene from
mesh surfaces and record their world-space 3D positions,
pixel projections, visibility, instance identities, and dy-
namic or static labels. Under the Blender camera conven-
tion, depth is defined as −zcam.
• Dense geometry. We export dense geometry at 250 Hz,
which is an integer divisor of the 1000 Hz HR rendering
rate. Thus, every dense timestamp coincides with a ren-
dered frame and requires no temporal interpolation. Each
dense frame contains depth and a per-pixel surface iden-
tity buffer comprising the instance ID, triangle ID, and
perspective-correct barycentric weights. Both are stored
at full resolution (256×256) and at 1/8 spatial resolution.
Nearest-neighbor subsampling preserves discrete IDs.
• Dense correspondences. We derive 2D and 3D scene
flow, target coordinates, and four-state labels (visible, oc-
cluded, out-of-frame, or invalid) analytically on demand
from the identity buffers. Each surface point is recon-
structed in object space, transformed into world coordi-
nates at the source and target times using the stored object
poses, and reprojected into the target view. Occlusion is
determined by a depth test against the target depth recon-
structed from the target pixel’s own identity buffer.
Data splits.
We generate datasets in tiers using determin-
istic seeds and partition them into training, validation, and
test sets with an 80/10/10 split.

D.3. Time Lens Dataset
The High-Speed Event and RGB (HS-ERGB) dataset, in-
troduced with Time Lens [46], contains real event streams
and high-speed color videos of dynamic indoor and out-
door scenes. The recording setup combines a Prophesee
Gen4 event camera (1280 × 720) with a global-shutter
FLIR Blackfly S RGB camera (1440 × 1080), separated
by a 2.5 cm baseline and synchronized in hardware. The
recorded sequences use RGB frame rates of 150–163 Hz.
They comprise close-range scenes with fast, nonlinear ob-
ject motion and distant scenes dominated by camera mo-
tion, including spinning objects, bursting balloons, water
splashes, and rapid camera rotations.
Spatial alignment uses stereo rectification, with an ad-
ditional feature-based homography for close-range planar
scenes recorded with a stationary camera. HS-ERGB com-
plements our synthetic benchmark with real sensor record-
ings of challenging motion. Its high-rate RGB frames pro-
vide image references for frame-interpolation evaluation;
the original release does not include annotated 2D/3D point
trajectories or independent-motion masks. It therefore sup-
ports qualitative assessment of our tracking and segmenta-
tion without providing ground truth for their endpoint errors
or mask IoU.
