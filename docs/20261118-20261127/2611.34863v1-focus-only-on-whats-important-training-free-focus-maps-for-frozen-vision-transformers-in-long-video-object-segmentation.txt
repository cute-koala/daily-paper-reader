Focus Only on What’s Important: Training-Free Focus Maps for
Frozen Vision Transformers in Long-Video Object Segmentation
Harsha Karipineni
Independent Researcher
boletoms@gmail.com
Rajesh Reddy Chintha
Independent Researcher

Abstract
Vision transformers are strong but costly image encoders for video object segmentation.
Training-free token merging cuts compute uniformly across the frame, including on tracked
objects. We give a frozen DINOv3 ViT inside a trained Cutie head a training-free focus map: full
resolution where the segmenter expects its objects and coarser merging elsewhere. Protecting only
the previous frame’s mask works on short clips but fails on long videos, where fast motion pushes
targets outside the margin and into background merging, locking in loss. Our policy instead takes
the union of velocity-shifted and block-matched masks, runs a camera-compensated surprise
check, delegates uncertain regions to the compared merger, and falls back to it when all objects
are lost. On long videos (LVOS V2 valid, pre-specified protocol, three ViT-S seeds), it beats
ALGM 0.98 by +2.76 J &F (95% object CI [+1.22, +4.44]; video-cluster [+0.99, +4.52]) at 16%
fewer counted encoder FLOPs (0.385 vs. 0.459), sitting 1.0 J &F point below the dense-encoder
baseline; at ViT-B the gain over ALGM 0.95 is +3.89 [+2.03, +5.87]. On DAVIS 2017 val it stays
within −0.19 J &F of the dense encoder at ViT-S (−0.12 at ViT-B). Controls qualify the result:
protecting only the previous mask under ALGM matches or exceeds accuracy at higher FLOPs
(0.486), so expectation and surprise mainly reduce counted encoder compute. Practically, lower
counted encoder compute did not reduce startup-inclusive mean Cutie step time on a 12-video
LVOS sample (3%–8% lower in steady state; an equally engineered ALGM is faster at ViT-S and
ViT-B). Section 5.2 separately examines a Cutie permanent-memory schedule and its latency
and memory trade-offs. Code: https://github.com/boletoms/focused-cv

Introduction
Frozen self-supervised vision transformers such as DINOv3 [36] give strong dense features, which
makes them attractive encoders for video object segmentation (VOS). Their cost grows with the
number of patch tokens, and VOS processes every frame at high resolution. Training-free token
reduction [2, 21, 22, 27, 39] lowers that cost by merging similar tokens, with one rule for the whole
image.
VOS offers something these methods do not use: the segmenter’s prediction for the previous
frame says where the objects are. A natural design keeps the predicted objects, plus a margin, at
full resolution and merges the rest hard. We call the per-patch map that decides how much compute
each patch gets a focus map, and its full-resolution patches the focus areas. On short DAVIS clips
the closed-loop focus map works: it comes within 1 J &F of dense at 0.29 of dense FLOPs, half of
what ALGM 0.98 uses (Section 5.6). On long videos it fails. Our diagnostics (Section 3.2) show
why: when a target moves more than two patches between frames, part of it falls outside the margin
and is merged with the background; the target is lost, and since the map follows the prediction, it
stays lost. On such frames the closed-loop map loses targets nine times as often as dense; away
from fast motion it loses them no more often than dense.

We propose to stop trusting the last mask: expect where each object will be, then check the
frame against that expectation. The method has four parts, all training-free (Figure 1):
1. Expectation. For each tracked object, take the union of its velocity-shifted and block-matched
masks (found on the current frame’s pooled pixels), grown by one patch (two at ViT-B). This
region runs at full resolution.
2. Surprise check. A per-patch mismatch between the current frame and the previous frame
shifted by the estimated camera motion, with a variance floor and a small spatial tolerance.
Patches that fire are promoted, never demoted.
3. Three tiers. The expectation (high tier) is never merged. Speed-scaled rings, search regions
around lost objects and surprise patches (middle tier) are merged by the compared merger’s
own rules. Everything else (low tier) is merged by an aggressive budget with a floor of context
tokens.
4. Fallback. When every object is lost, the frame runs the compared merger unchanged.
Our contributions are:
• A diagnosis of why closed-loop focus maps fail on long videos: fast motion causes compression-
induced losses that the loop locks in.
• A training-free focus policy that runs at inference time in a trained Cutie head receiving
stride-16 features from frozen DINOv3 (tested with one segmenter, Cutie, and one backbone
family, DINOv3), conditioning compute on an expectation of object and camera motion, a
surprise check, and a fallback to the compared merger.
• A pre-specified evaluation: the rule for every adoption decision was written in dated plan
files before the run that tested it, and the long-video test pools three heads (one base head
fine-tuned with three seeds), at ViT-S and again at ViT-B. We report every run, including
failures.
• Ablations showing that full resolution in the expected region is necessary, and an exact
attribution of where the long-video gain comes from.
• A training-free permanent-memory schedule for Cutie (a finding about the segmenter, not the
focus map) and, with it, operating points at which the encoder runs at about a third of dense
FLOPs at or above dense accuracy on held-out long videos (Section 5.2).
• A graph-compatible implementation that takes the same decisions as the reference imple-
mentation and turns the FLOPs saving into wall-clock speed-ups over dense, with the same
engineering applied to the strongest baseline.

Related work
Training-free token reduction for dense prediction.
ToMe [2] merges the most similar
token pairs by bipartite matching in every block. ALGM [27] merges locally in 2 × 2 windows
early and globally later with a similarity threshold, designed for segmentation with plain ViTs.
AiluRus [21] adapts resolution to content, PiToMe [39] preserves the spectrum of the token graph,
and Expedite [22] clusters tokens and reconstructs a dense output. G2TM [1] coarsens a graph of
spatially adjacent tokens and SCMerge [32] penalises pair merges that break spatial consistency,
both for semantic segmentation; DTEM [20], unlike the others, learns its merging embeddings. Each
applies one rule to the whole image. We port ToMe, ALGM, AiluRus, PiToMe and Expedite from
2


their authors’ code and compare them inside the same VOS model at operating points fixed in
advance (Section 4). Content-aware token sharing [23] lets image regions share tokens by content and
dynamic token pruning [37] drops easy tokens in plain-ViT segmenters, both image-level. MPM [30]
pairs mutual nearest neighbours and CubistMerge [15] keeps the spatial layout so that merging
works with window attention and rotary embeddings; both are image-level and use no information
from earlier frames.
Prompt- and region-aware reduction.
Segment This Thing [34] tokenizes finely around a point
prompt and coarsely elsewhere, the closest image-level analogue of a focus map, without a previous
frame; StructSAM [26] protects boundary and prompt regions when merging in SAM; PrATo [12]
prunes by a prompt-derived spatial prior; VLTP [3] prunes by language guidance. In video, for SAM
2 [31], Fast SAM2 [25] prunes encoder outputs by text before propagation; Efficient-SAM2 [43] keeps
full encoder computation in the windows covered by the previous frame’s dilated predicted masks,
adds salient windows when tracking confidence is low, and routes the rest through a light branch
trained by feature reconstruction; the Recurrent Dynamic Submodel [38] chooses SAM 2’s blocks per
frame with a learned router that reads the previous mask and the current features. Motion-aware
ROI prediction [13] forecasts a target’s crop from its trajectory. Video Patch Pruning [14] learns
to select patches early from the previous frame’s features for video instance segmentation, and
MaskVD [33] masks regions for video detection without training, refreshing full frames periodically.
RS3-Prune [24] prunes the readout queries and memory writes of memory-bank VOS without
training. Our closed-loop focus map belongs to the family that uses the previous prediction as
the prompt. The method we propose differs in where the full-resolution region comes from (an
expectation of object and camera motion, corrected on the current frame), in its surprise check, in
giving an uncertain middle tier to the compared merger’s own rules, in falling back to that merger
when tracking fails, and in its analysis of long videos, where we find that the plain closed-loop map
fails.
Temporal reuse.
Eventful Transformers [11] and related methods reuse computation for un-
changed tokens across frames, and temporal cluster assignment [42] reuses token clusters. Run-length
tokenization [7] drops patches repeated over time and names camera motion as its failure mode, and
vid-TLDR [6] merges background tokens of video transformers by attention-based saliency. A reuse
option we built in the same codebase, in the style of Eventful Transformers (our implementation,
not the authors’ code; skipped patches keep their cached output and serve as keys and values), saved
at most 2% of FLOPs within one J &F point of the budget alone on DAVIS 2017 val (30 videos,
closed-loop maps), and 10 to 15% at a cost of 1.2 to 1.4 points: after the budget, low-interest areas
are merged to a floor, and handheld camera motion changes almost every patch. It was not run on
long videos or with the expectation focus map. We reduce tokens within each frame; the focus map
uses the previous frames only to decide where. Long-video segmenters address memory rather than
encoder cost: XMem [4] and Cutie [5] keep long-term memories, and SAM2Long [10] keeps several
memory pathways without training. Pollard and Wray [28] study training-free token merging in
video transformers, which merge tokens across space and time with one rule for the whole clip, and
report about 2.5 times the speed at a small accuracy cost on action recognition.
Memory-based VOS.
Cutie [5] reads object-level memory to segment each frame; LVOS [17]
measures long-term VOS, where losses and re-appearances dominate. We keep Cutie unchanged
except its pixel encoder, whose stride-16 features come from the frozen ViT under each method;
its memory readout and decoder are identical for every method and excluded from the compute
3


we report. For SAM 2, SAM2Dual [19] adds a training-free long-term memory filled by interval
sampling to a short-term one, a memory schedule in the spirit of Appendix D but for a different
segmenter and without an encoder-compute saving; and the codec-guided compressor of Sheng et
al. [35], for video language models, uses compressed-video motion vectors as a prior for merging
tokens across frames, which is temporal and training-free but not tied to a segmenter’s predicted
objects. SAMURAI [41] adds a training-free Kalman motion model to SAM 2’s memory selection
for tracking, and EdgeTAM [44] and EfficientTAM [40] replace SAM 2’s image encoder by lighter
trained ones and also make its memory attention cheaper; none of them changes the encoder’s
compute per frame from the segmenter’s predictions.

Figure 1: The focus map. Before the encoder runs on frame t, for each tracked object we take
the union of its velocity-shifted and block-matched masks on frame t (high tier). A surprise check
compares frame t with frame t−1 shifted by the camera motion and promotes patches that changed
without explanation. Rings sized by each object’s speed and search regions around lost objects form

3.1
Setting
The encoder is a frozen DINOv3 ViT (ViT-S/16; ViT-B/16 and ViT-L/16 as checks) with 4 register
tokens and RoPE positions. A method may merge patch tokens between attention and MLP in any
block. Each token carries its size (how many original patches it holds), a size-weighted position
used for RoPE, and its tier. After the last block every original patch takes the output of the token
that holds it (unmerge), so Cutie’s decoder receives a full stride-16 feature map. The ViT is never
trained; Cutie’s remaining modules are trained once per head with the ViT dense (Section 4).
3.2
Why a closed-loop focus map fails on long videos
The closed-loop focus map protects every patch the previous prediction touches, grown by one patch
(two at ViT-B), and merges the rest with a budget: in every block bipartite matching merges the
remaining low-tier tokens among themselves at a nominal rate of 0.7, which pairing caps at one half,
4


and never below 64 of them remain. It comes within 1 J &F of dense on DAVIS at 0.29 of dense
FLOPs (Table 5) but falls below ALGM on long videos (paired differences from −5.38 to −0.10
J &F across the heads we tested on LVOS V1 val).
Logged runs on the development split (LVOS V2 train) locate the cause. The share of a tracked
target outside the protected patches grows with its displacement since the previous frame: 0.6%
under one patch, 12.1% at 2 to 4 patches, 31.5% beyond 4 patches. LVOS runs at 6 frames per
second, so this lag is geometric and the same for every map source, including the previous ground
truth. On frames where a target moves more than two patches (about 8% of tracked frames), the
closed-loop map loses it on 2.81% of frames, against 0.30% for dense and 0.36% for ALGM (each
rate counts the frames on which that method was still tracking the target, so the three come from
different sets of frames); on all other frames the three are equal (0.01 to 0.03%). Ninety-six percent
of its loss onsets fall on fast frames. Losses are not always permanent (92% of the closed-loop
map’s visible-loss onsets recovered, 741 of 809, on the development split), but the deficit against
ALGM sits in the frames where the closed-loop map is lost and ALGM tracks. The losses are
compression-induced (the lagging part of the target is merged with background), and the loop locks
them in. Remedies tried on the closed-loop map, a dense I-frame every 5 frames and dense I-frames
triggered by evidence of the target outside the map, helped on development videos but not on LVOS
V1 val; this led to the design below, which replaces full resets by an expectation, a surprise check
and the fallback.
3.3
Expectation (high tier)
For each object we maintain a run-time state: its last mask, centroid, and velocity estimated from
predictions at t−1 and t−2, together with a separate history of up to 10 tracked-mask areas. In the
runtime controller, an object counts as lost when its predicted mask area falls below a tenth of the
median of those last 10 tracked areas, or below 16 pixels (distinct from the ground-truth IoU < 0.1
loss definition used in the outcome analysis of Section 5.3). For each tracked object, we take the
union of the velocity-shifted and block-matched masks—(a) its last mask shifted by its centroid
velocity and (b) its last mask shifted by the displacement that best matches its template between
the previous and the current frame on 4 × 4-pooled pixels (search within 8 patches)—and grow that
union by one patch (two at ViT-B). Object masks are taken at patch level (a patch belongs to the
mask at 1% coverage; the template uses the patches covered at least half, or the mask if there are
none). The high tier is the union over objects, plus the previous prediction and its velocity shift;
before the expected regions are added, the base map from the previous prediction is rounded up to
the next multiple of 64 patches with its nearest unprotected patches (at most 63 extra, as for the
closed-loop map). The high tier is never merged, although its tokens still attend to merged ones.
Offline on 420 development videos, this expectation covers 93.3% of fast-moving targets against
77.8% for the one-patch margin, with 31% more area on those frames (10% over all frames); it was
chosen by a rule fixed before that measurement.
3.4
Surprise check
A surprise is any deviation from the expected frame. For each patch p outside the expectation we
compare the current frame’s pooled pixels at p with the previous frame’s at p −g, where g is the
integer camera shift (within 4 patches) that best aligns the two frames. The mismatch is normalised
per patch with a variance floor σ = 0.05 and takes the best of nine offsets of up to 4 pixels; a patch
fires at e ≥0.06, the highest threshold that reached 95% recall on both offline event sets (targets
entering outside the map, and re-appearances). Content entering at the image border always fires.
5


A fired patch inside an object’s ring or search region joins the high tier; any other fired patch joins
the middle tier. If more than half of the patches outside the base map fire (a frame-wide change
such as a cut or fast camera motion), all of them join the middle tier while the expectation stays
whole. Surprise only promotes: false positives cost compute, false negatives cost accuracy, and we
prefer the former.
3.5
Three tiers
• High: the expectation and promoted surprise patches; full resolution.
• Middle: a ring of 1 + max(1, ⌈s⌉) patches around each expectation (s: speed in patches per
frame); for each lost object, its last tracked mask moved by n times its last velocity and grown
by 2 + n patches, n frames after the loss; surprise patches not in the high tier. Merged by the
compared merger’s own rules: for ALGM, its layer-1 2 × 2 windows (only windows without a
high-tier patch) and its threshold matching between non-high tokens in layer 5 (layer 7 in the
24-block ViT-L).
• Low: everything else, merged by ALGM’s rules where they apply and then by the budget
(nominal rate 0.7 per block, capped at one half by pairing; floor 64 tokens at ViT-S, 128 at
ViT-B and ViT-L). A merge of middle and low tokens stays middle, so the budget never
reaches the middle tier.
All tier decisions are made before the first merge, while every patch is still a token. The focus areas
are the high- and middle-tier patches.
3.6
Fallback
When every object that has appeared is lost, or the map is empty, the frame runs the compared
merger exactly (plain ALGM 0.98 at ViT-S); the expectation observes passively so its state stays
current. On the held-out protocol the fallback runs on 29.6% of frames (three heads); Section 5.3
shows the gain is not produced by these frames.
3.7
Compute accounting
We count whole-encoder FLOPs per frame from what the forward pass did: projections on the
tokens entering attention, attention per computed query–key pair, MLP on the tokens left after
merging, the patch embedding, and every operation a method adds (matching, clustering, and our
feature products for the camera search, block matching and the surprise mismatch). Norms, softmax
and RoPE are omitted for every method alike. We report the ratio to dense, averaged per frame
(frame-weighted across videos); dense frames inside a method’s run count 1.0. Cutie’s memory and
decoder are identical across methods and excluded. The count follows the tokens a method keeps;
the timed implementation’s padded buffers and redone blocks are overhead of that implementation
and appear only in wall clock. Wall clock is measured separately (Section 5.8).
3.8
Timed implementation
A direct implementation is bound by the host: per-frame Python work, host reads of token counts
and many small kernels outweigh the work it removes. With the method unchanged, the timed
implementation keeps every step before the encoder on the GPU (two host reads per frame), sizes
each block’s token buffer from the previous frame’s counts (a frame that exceeds a buffer is redone
from that block on, so outputs never depend on the guess), captures every step in CUDA graphs,
6


compiles each block segment, uses variable-length flash attention [8], and overlaps block 0’s attention
with the tier planning. It equals its own eager form bit for bit, rounds every bf16 intermediate as
the reference path does, and takes the reference path’s decisions: replaying the ground-truth masks
of all 140 long videos through both, the fallback decision agrees on every frame and the tiers on
all but one of 61,880 frames (an fp32 centroid at exactly half a pixel; Appendix A). Every number
of the method in Tables 1–7 comes from this implementation unless marked; baselines run their
ported code.

Experimental setup
Model.
Cutie (small) with its pixel encoder’s stride-16 features replaced by the frozen DINOv3
ViT under each method; the stride-8 and stride-4 skips come from Cutie’s own ResNet-18 stem.
Heads (Cutie’s modules other than the ViT) are trained with the ViT frozen and dense on DAVIS
2017 train and MOSE train [9] with Cutie’s training script; we do not train on YouTube-VOS or
static images ourselves. Each head starts from the official Cutie small weights (cutie-small-mega;
all non-ViT tensors except the two new stride-16 projections), which their authors trained with
static-image pre-training and on DAVIS, YouTube-VOS, MOSE, BURST and OVIS, so our heads
inherit that training; trains the projections for 5,000 iterations and then everything for 40,000 (batch
2). The long-video heads are fine-tuned from one such base head for 10,000 iterations with three
Cutie seeds, so they share the base head’s 45,000 iterations: R31c, h2 and h3 at ViT-S, and R41 b1
to b3 (from head R25) at ViT-B. ViT-L has one head.
Data.
DAVIS 2017 val [29] (30 videos, 61 objects, 480p) for short clips. For the held-out long-video
test, LVOS V2 valid [18]: 140 videos with 239 objects, made of LVOS V1 val [17] (50 videos, 65
objects; its frames and annotations are identical in V2) and 90 videos new in V2 (174 objects). The
method’s components and settings were chosen on LVOS V2 train; the 50 V1 videos had, however,
been used earlier to evaluate the closed-loop design and its variants (whose failure there motivated
this work), so only the 90 new videos were untouched before this test, and we report them separately.
Throughout, “held-out” means not used to choose the method’s components or thresholds in the
first place; LVOS V2 valid was nevertheless evaluated in many pre-specified passes (Appendices A
and B list them), and later choices made after the main result (the surprise threshold 0.5, the
schedule operating points) were proposed after earlier held-out results had been seen. LVOS V2
train (420 videos) for development: diagnostics, gates and offline measurements. Cutie’s LVOS
protocol: shortest side 480, long-term memory, each object’s mask given at its first appearance.
Metrics.
J &F per object, under two scorers. Ours scores every annotated frame after an object’s
first appearance at 1280 × 720, with a boundary tolerance of 0.8% of the diagonal; all decisions
in this paper were made with it. The official toolkits [17, 29], run unchanged on the saved masks,
score each object within its annotated range without its first and last frame (LVOS) or every frame
but the first and last (DAVIS). We report both.
Baselines.
ToMe, ALGM, AiluRus, PiToMe and Expedite, ported from the authors’ code at
recorded commits and tested against the originals. Map-aware “protected” variants of ALGM keep
the closed-loop focus map at full resolution and apply ALGM elsewhere. Operating points follow
rules fixed in advance (for example ALGM’s threshold closest to a target FLOPs on DAVIS: 0.98 at
ViT-S, 0.95 at ViT-B and ViT-L).

Protocol and statistics.
Paired differences per object with 95% bootstrap intervals (1,000
resamples of objects). Objects of one video share frames and tracker state, so Appendix B also
reports intervals that resample whole videos (10,000 resamples) and the mean that weights videos
equally, as sensitivity checks; the decision rule fixed in advance uses the object bootstrap. The long-
video test was fixed before any run: per object, the difference against ALGM averaged over the three
heads, then bootstrapped; superiority if the lower bound is above 0 at lower FLOPs, non-inferiority
if above −3.0. This primary comparison is not adjusted; the secondary comparisons (per head,
per split, and the method against each short-clip baseline) are reported with Holm-adjusted [16]
two-sided p-values per family from a null-centred bootstrap over whole videos (10,000 resamples;
Appendix B). Every adoption rule (expectation variant, surprise threshold, gates, validation) was
written in the project’s dated plan files before the run that tested it (pre-specified, not registered
externally). Evaluations use deterministic GPU kernels where PyTorch provides them; the timed
implementation’s compiled kernels are not bitwise reproducible across runs (Appendix B). Attention
runs in bf16 or fp32, never fp16.

Ablations
• Merging the expected region costs accuracy. Merging the high tier by ALGM’s rules
(everything else unchanged; reference implementation) lowers FLOPs by 10% (0.348 against
0.388) and costs −5.13 J &F [−6.91, −3.48] on the held-out protocol, which puts it below
plain ALGM (−2.23 [−3.54, −0.93]). The budget changes with it, so this shows that ALGM’s
merging there hurts, not that every protected patch is needed. On the development split, with
an earlier surprise threshold, the same ablation costs −2.47 [−3.50, −1.56].
• Against the closed-loop focus map. Against the closed-loop map, which has no expectation
or surprise check and falls back to ToMe r64 when its map is empty (so this compares the
whole design, not the expectation alone), the method is +3.79 J &F on all 420 development
videos and +6.89 on the fast-motion subset, at 0.377 against 0.268 FLOPs.
• Against the previous prediction under ALGM. Keeping the closed-loop map at full
resolution with plain ALGM 0.98 elsewhere and as the fallback, with no expectation or surprise
check, matches the method’s accuracy on the 420 development videos (+1.36 against +1.33
J &F over ALGM 0.98) but at 0.505 against 0.377 FLOPs. On the 140 held-out videos (three
heads) it is +3.45 [+2.01, +5.03] over ALGM 0.98 and +0.74 [−0.33, +1.90] over the method, at
0.486 FLOPs against the method’s 0.386 and ALGM’s 0.459; on the 90 new videos it is ahead
of the method (+1.61 [+0.42, +2.95]), and on the 42 objects in videos longer than 600 frames
the two are level (+0.17 [−2.44, +2.63]). This control shows how much accuracy protecting
the previous mask under ALGM already achieves at higher compute (0.486 FLOPs); the
expectation and surprise tiers operate at lower counted encoder FLOPs (0.386) with a small
accuracy trade-off, though this comparison cannot assign the gain to individual components.
• How much surprise is needed. Raising the surprise threshold from 0.06 to 0.5 (reference
implementation, held-out protocol, ablation after the main run) lowers FLOPs from 0.388 to
0.313 (19%) at −0.06 J &F [−1.10, +1.00] against the method, and stays ahead of ALGM 0.98
(+2.84 [+1.16, +4.70], against 0.459 FLOPs); turning the rule off gives −1.10 [−2.38, +0.12] at
0.307 FLOPs (+1.80 [+0.04, +3.68] over ALGM). The setting was chosen on the development
split by a rule fixed in advance (it passed there: +0.05 at 0.269 against 0.366 FLOPs); on the
held-out videos its lower bound is −1.10, short of the −1.0 the rule required, so it is reported
as an ablation and not adopted. On the development split, dropping the ring as well costs
−1.73 [−3.77, −0.15]: the ring and the surprise rule substitute for each other. The surprise
check at its tuned threshold therefore buys little accuracy for about a fifth of the FLOPs.
12


• Headroom of the map’s inputs. Giving the method a perfect tracker’s last two masks (the
ground truth of the two previous frames, for the expectation and for the map; a diagnostic,
not a method) on 104 development videos changes J &F by +0.11 [−0.09, +0.42] at 0.364
against 0.366 FLOPs (+0.33 [−0.06, +1.03] on the 44 long videos, +0.40 [−0.09, +1.31] on the
fast-motion stratum). The map’s inputs are therefore not what limits the method.
• Noise in the surprise check. The variance floor and the 4-pixel tolerance halve false
frame-wide surprises from sensor noise (12.8% against 30.3% of frames); camera compensation
finer than one patch did not reduce them further.

A second baseline: ToMe
We applied the same design with ToMe as the compared merger (ToMe’s matching on the middle
tier, plain ToMe r64 as the fallback). The first version is −2.26 J &F [−3.93, −0.71] against plain
ToMe r64 at 0.449 against 0.709 FLOPs on the held-out protocol, so it is not non-inferior: ToMe
merges a fixed 64 tokens per block, and once the budget has thinned the low tier those merges fall
on the middle tier. A second version (ToMe’s rule as a rate on the tiers) failed its development
gate and was not validated. A third version adds two rules aimed at the failure modes found on
15


development videos: a frame whose tracked object moves at the block-matching radius runs plain
ToMe, and frames Cutie writes to memory run plain ToMe. It passed its gate and on the held-out
protocol is non-inferior at 21% lower FLOPs (−0.68 [−1.56, +0.10] at 0.557 against 0.709), not
superior. Both numbers are from a rerun after a fix: the fallback frames of the first run let the
focus map’s interest leak into ToMe’s merging (Appendix B), and the first-run values were −2.23
and −0.45. The held-out videos judged the ToMe arm twice, and both revisions were designed on
the development split after the first failure; we report every attempt. With ToMe the design saves
compute at ToMe’s accuracy; with ALGM it also gains accuracy.

Limitations
• Second baseline. Against ToMe the design is non-inferior at lower FLOPs, not superior,
and only after two revisions made after a held-out failure.
• Wall clock. The encoder is faster than dense in steady state, but Cutie’s whole step falls by
only 3% to 8% in closed loop on LVOS and, with per-video startup, is not faster than dense
on videos of about 540 frames (Section 5.8); at ViT-S and ViT-B the encoder is slower than
ALGM’s given the same engineering; with deterministic kernels the ViT-S speed-up over dense
is small and ALGM is faster at every size. The ViT-L timed path has an unresolved intermittent
crash, with the overlap on and off, that required relaunching long runs (Appendix B); the
ViT-L timing (114 frames) completed with the overlap on.
• Fallback share. On about 30% of long-video frames, the policy switches to plain ALGM
merging. Cutie continues from the memory accumulated on preceding frames.
• Per-split power and the gap to dense. On LVOS V1 val alone (65 objects) the interval
crosses 0, at ViT-S and at ViT-B; on the new V2 videos the method is below dense while
above ALGM. At ViT-B the method stays 3.2 J &F below dense.
• Very long videos and lost targets. The gain over ALGM comes from videos up to
600 frames (Table 10: +3.60 up to 300 frames, +2.95 from 301 to 600 at ViT-S, reference
implementation); on the 42 objects in videos longer than 600 frames it is not significant. In
the timed-implementation runs, which count the three 300-frame videos in the middle bin (300
to 600 frames inclusive), the middle bin gives +4.35 at ViT-S and +4.83 at ViT-B per object
and the longer videos +0.48 (ViT-S), +0.89 (ViT-B) and +0.32 (ViT-L). Where the fallback
runs on more than half of a video’s frames, the method is level with ALGM (+0.06, −0.36,
−1.92); these videos are grouped by an outcome of the method, so this shows where it helps,
not why. Writing every 20th frame to Cutie’s permanent memory lifts every encoder on these
videos by 10 to 12 J &F (Appendix D); it is a property of Cutie’s memory, so it is reported
as an additional operating point (Section 5.2) and not as part of the method. Keeping the
gain on the longest videos is the next design step.
• Backbone trend on V1 val. On the 50 V1 videos the gain over ALGM shrinks as the
backbone grows (+3.62, +0.88, −1.51 at ViT-S, B, L; 65 objects, every interval crosses 0); on
the 90 new videos it does not (+2.43, +5.01, +3.36).
• Motion model. The camera estimate is one integer translation and block matching searches
an axis-aligned window, so rotation, zoom, parallax, blur and repeated texture can mislead
the expectation or the surprise check; the fallback changes the merger but cannot undo what
earlier frames wrote to Cutie’s memory.
16


• Scope. One VOS model (Cutie), one backbone family (DINOv3), one head at ViT-L. The
frozen-ViT Cutie is below the official Cutie on LVOS (official small 65.99 and base 69.86 on
V1 val, 65 objects; our ViT-S head −11.37 [−18.58, −4.28] below the small model, our ViT-B
head −3.28 [−9.17, +2.43] below the small and −7.15 [−13.03, −1.59] below the base model;
earlier heads, one run), which lowers every method’s absolute numbers alike. Its heads start
from the official Cutie weights and so inherit their training data (Section 4).
• Reuse of data.
The method and its gate subset were informed by diagnostics on the
development split, and its second gate run reused that split after a failed first run. The
held-out test ran once on the reference implementation with rules fixed before it; the timed
implementation was then evaluated on the same videos four times, each rerun following an
implementation fix found by comparing decisions with the reference path, never by accuracy,
with the rules unchanged. All five runs are superior to ALGM 0.98 (+2.63 to +2.90 J &F),
and all are reported (Appendix A); a sixth, the rerun that saved masks for the official toolkit,
gives +2.71 (Appendix B).
• Schedule operating points. The rows with the permanent-memory schedule (Section 5.2)
use a Cutie-level change that raises Cutie’s step time (median +49%, 90th percentile 17.5
to 47.9 ms) and peak GPU memory (1.08 to 6.45 GiB peak allocated over 12 videos, dense
Cutie), neither of which the encoder FLOPs count. The threshold-0.5 variant was chosen on
the development split among five combinations and run once on held-out videos that had
already served the method; its intervals against dense Cutie with the same schedule include
zero. The schedule was not run on a third, untouched split; the LVOS test server, which would
provide one, was not used.
• Short clips. On DAVIS the closed-loop focus map is cheaper at dense level; the method
trades short-clip compute for long-video robustness.
7
Conclusion
A frozen DINOv3 ViT inside a trained Cutie head can spend its compute where the segmenter
expects the objects to be. Under the pre-specified long-video protocol (LVOS V2 valid, three heads
at ViT-S and ViT-B), full resolution in the expected region, the compared merger’s own rules in an
uncertain middle tier, and fallback when tracking fails beat ALGM at lower counted encoder FLOPs
(+2.76 J &F at 16% fewer FLOPs at ViT-S; +3.89 at ViT-B) and stay within 0.2 J &F of the dense
encoder on DAVIS 2017 val. The prior-mask ALGM control shows how much accuracy full resolution
on the previous prediction already achieves at higher compute (0.486 FLOPs), while the expectation
and surprise tiers operate below ALGM’s FLOPs with a small accuracy trade-off. Separately, a
training-free permanent-memory schedule for Cutie lifts every encoder on long videos (reaching
73.61 J &F at 0.335 encoder FLOPs with τ = 0.5, level with scheduled dense), at a +49% median
step-time and 6× GPU-memory cost (Section 5.2, Appendix D). Finally, counted encoder FLOPs do
not translate into wall-clock savings on our 12-video LVOS sample (mean startup-inclusive step time
is above dense Cutie, and an equally engineered ALGM is faster at ViT-S and ViT-B; Section 5.8).
Speed beyond an equally engineered ALGM at small backbones, and superiority over a second
baseline, remain open.

A permanent-memory schedule for Cutie
This appendix reports a finding about Cutie’s memory, not about the focus map, and the controls
that show it. Cutie writes a working-memory frame every fifth frame and evicts old ones; masks
given by the user are permanent. On long videos the object is lost for want of old, clean anchors.
The schedule writes every 20th frame, with Cutie’s own predicted mask, to the permanent memory
(the method also runs that frame at full resolution, as an I-frame; dense Cutie runs every frame at
full resolution anyway). Periodic and permanent memories are not new in themselves: XMem [4]
and Cutie keep the given annotation permanently, and SAM2Dual [19] fills a long-term memory
by interval sampling; what we report is the effect of a 20-frame permanent write of Cutie’s own
predictions, with controls on three encoders. No training; each object receives its standard first-
appearance annotation, and scheduled writes store the model’s own predictions. It was chosen on
the development split (420 LVOS V2 train videos, one head) from seven frame schedules (a dense or
fallback frame every 10, 20 or 30 frames, or on loss; none passed its pre-specified gate alone) and
three memory treatments (forced write, clearing then writing, permanent write; only the permanent
write passed), and then run once on the held-out videos with all three heads. Because the held-out
gain was far larger than the development gain and above dense, controls were added: plain ALGM
0.98 and dense Cutie with the same schedule on the same videos.

F
Settings
ViT-S: high tier full resolution; middle tier under ALGM 0.98’s rules; low tier budget at nominal rate
0.7 (capped at one half per block by pairing), floor 64 tokens; margin 1 patch; base map rounded
up to 64-patch size classes; fallback plain ALGM 0.98. ALGM merges locally in layer 1 and globally
in layer 5 (ViT-S, ViT-B) or 7 (ViT-L). ViT-B: ALGM 0.95, floor 128, margin 2. ViT-L: ALGM
0.95, floor 128, margin 1. Expectation and surprise settings are pixel-based and identical across
backbones: block-matching radius 8 patches on 4 × 4-pooled pixels, camera search within 4 patches,
variance floor 0.05 (with 10−5 added), the best of nine offsets in {−4, 0, 4}2 pixels, threshold 0.06,
frame-wide rule when more than half of the patches outside the base map fire, object masks at 1%
patch coverage and templates at 50%, velocity shifts rounded to whole patches, lost below a tenth
of the median of the last 10 tracked areas or 16 pixels.
