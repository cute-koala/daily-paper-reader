Abstract
Modern video object segmentation (VOS) relies on a two-stage inference paradigm:
target initialization and memory-based propagation. However, existing adversar-
ial attacks on VOS often overlook this structure. To bridge this gap, we pro-
pose AUV, an adversarial framework that Aligns with the Universal two-stage
paradigm of VOS models. Specifically, AUV optimizes a universal adversarial
perturbation (UAP) through decoupled training strategies tailored for both ini-
tialization and propagation stages. By disrupting the shared memory space, it
achieves prompt-agnostic attacks across different modalities, including masks,
points, boxes, and language. Extensive experiments show that AUV establishes
state-of-the-art VOS attack performance, such as degrading mask-based SAM3 to
only 8.4 J &F on DAVIS17-val (compared to 55.4 for the previous SOTA under
the same training setup). It also exhibits superior temporal dynamics with the
fastest onset, the most enduring persistence, and the support of any-time mid-video
attacks. The AUV optimization framework is applicable to different VOS mod-
els, revealing vulnerabilities in real-world streaming VOS. Code is available at
https://github.com/FudanCVL/AUV.

Introduction
Modern video object segmentation (VOS) [37, 4, 40, 2] systems follow a two-stage inference
paradigm. In the first stage, the target is specified through a prompt, such as a mask, point, box, or
language. In the second stage, the model propagates the target across frames using memory, with
little or no further prompting. This two-stage design is foundational to recent VOS models such as
XMem [6], Cutie [4], SAM2 [40], SAM3 [2], and is central to their robust performance.
Meanwhile, despite the rapid development of adversarial attacks, including those targeting VOS
models [24, 60, 28, 54], existing methods suffer from a fundamental training-inference misalign-
ment: they typically optimize the attacks under a single-stage formulation. Specifically, they either
focus on the prompt-conditioned initial frame while neglecting memory-based temporal propaga-
tion [28, 24], or apply the same optimization objective for all frames during training [60, 54]. This
mismatch between a uniform attack optimization strategy and the two-stage VOS inference
pipeline often leads to two critical limitations: (1) the attacks do not effectively corrupt latent
memory representations stored in the memory bank, potentially allowing the model to recover the
target during propagation; (2) they often target the predicted mask, a prompt-biased by-product in
VOS. Consequently, attacks optimized with sparse points or boxes often struggle to transfer to other
prompts encoded by different branches, such as dense masks or language.

Motivated by these observations, we propose AUV, a stage-Aligned Universal VOS attack framework
that optimizes a universal adversarial perturbation (UAP) to disrupt both the initialization and
propagation stages of the target VOS model. As illustrated in Figure 1 (left), AUV decouples
the attack optimization into two complementary stages that align with the VOS inference pipeline.
During prompt-based initialization, it optimizes the UAP to corrupt the target’s memory feature
anchor before it is permanently stored in the memory bank, thereby erasing the target identity at
its source. During memory-based propagation, it optimizes the same UAP to flatten the spatial
saliency of memory-attended feature maps, pushing their activations toward spatial uniformity and
thereby severing temporal target association across frames.
Our paradigm-aligned design not only delivers stronger attacks, with faster onset and more sustained
temporal persistence, but also supports a broader range of prompt modalities and more flexible
attack timing, as illustrated in Figure 1 (right). First, by targeting the shared latent memory space,
AUV avoids prompt-specific optimization and transfers zero-shot across different prompt modalities,
including masks, points, boxes, and language. Second, by targeting the propagation stage, AUV
supports any-time mid-video attacks, where the perturbation can be injected from any intermediate
frame and remains effective throughout the subsequent sequence.
Extensive experiments validate our design. Under the same training setup and perturbation budget,
AUV consistently outperforms prior attacks across VOS benchmarks and prompt modalities on SAM3,
with the performance gap even more pronounced during pure memory-based propagation. Moreover,
the optimization framework of AUV applies naturally to different VOS models, suggesting it exploits
shared properties of modern VOS systems, rather than a model- or prompt-specific weakness. The
main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Structural Alignment: We identify a fundamental mismatch between existing uniform
attack optimizations and the two-stage VOS inference pipeline. To address this, we propose
AUV, a stage-aligned UAP-based attack framework that simultaneously corrupts memory
anchor during initialization and flattens memory-attended features during propagation.
• Prompt-Agnostic Attack: Our attack targets the shared latent memory space rather than
prompt-conditioned output masks, enabling zero-shot generalization across different prompt
modalities, including mask, point, box, and language.
• Metrics & Empirical Insights: We introduce new metrics to evaluate the onset speed
and persistence of video attacks. Extensive experiments show AUV’s new SOTA attack
performance, while further analyses provide insights into its underlying mechanisms.

Related works
2.1
Promptable Video Segmentation
Promptable Video Segmentation focuses on segmenting explicitly specified target(s) within videos,
which includes Video Object Segmentation (VOS) [37, 53, 48, 55, 8, 27, 6, 5, 4], Promptable Concept
Segmentation (PCS) [2, 14, 12, 11, 29, 17, 16, 15, 41, 26, 44], and interactive video segmentation
[7, 34, 52, 40, 43]. Regardless of their differences, these tasks follow a consistent two-stage
inference paradigm: prompt-based target initialization and memory-based temporal propagation.
The former processes diverse prompts such as masks, points, boxes, and language to encode target
identity, while the latter propagates memory-conditioned target information throughout the sequence
for consistent video segmentation. This shared structure motivates an attack design tailored to the
distinct roles of initialization and propagation.
2.2
Adversarial Attacks on Segmentation Models
Adversarial attacks on segmentation models add pixel-level perturbations to input images to disrupt
segmentation predictions. Existing image-level methods [22] either disrupt image features to erode
target identity [25, 58], or suppress output masks through tailored strategies [57, 61, 21, 45, 3, 42].
Recently, adversarial attacks have been extended to video segmentation models [24, 60, 28, 54],
either targeting only the initial frame [28, 24] or using a shared optimization objective for all frames
within the video sequence [54, 60].
Meanwhile, recent works have also explored “cross-prompt” attacks with promising results [60, 30,
23]. However, cross-prompt transferability demonstrated with points or boxes does not necessarily
imply generalization to other prompt modalities, particularly dense masks or language, which are
processed through distinct encoding pathways. In this work, we aim for a prompt-agnostic attack that
remains effective regardless of prompt modalities by corrupting the shared latent memory space.

Design principle.
The overview of our method is shown in Figure 2. We propose AUV, whose
optimization aligns with the two-stage VOS inference pipeline by decoupling the UAP training into
two distinct stages that directly mirror Φinit and Φprop:
• Stage I - Initialization-aligned corruption. We corrupt the initial memory feature ˜E1
before it is permanently stored in B as an anchor, erasing the target identity at its source.
The target can be specified at initialization using a prompt P of any modality.
• Stage II - Propagation-aligned flattening. We flatten the spatial saliency of memory-
attended feature maps ˜Ai in subsequent frames, disrupting the temporal chain of target
propagation. The prompt encoder is disabled, consistent with VOS inference where no
prompts are provided after initialization. The same UAP δ is shared across both stages.

3.5
Joint Two-Stage Optimization
Staged curriculum.
Directly optimizing the combined loss from scratch is unstable: if the memory
anchor E1 is not yet corrupted, Stage II will have to disrupt propagation that still carries a strong
clean target signal. We therefore adopt a two-phase curriculum that follows the causal dependency
between initialization and propagation.
1. Phase I. Optimize δ using only Linit on the initial frame, without propagation, to establish a
corrupted initial memory anchor ˜E1.
2. Phase II. Extend training to full video sequence. The initial frame continues to be supervised
by Linit, while all subsequent frames are jointly penalized by L(i)

Experiments
4.1
Implementation Details
Following prior works [35, 10, 61, 60], we set the perturbation bound to ϵ = 10/255 for all methods.
We also provide results with smaller ϵ in the appendix. Following UAP-SAM2 [60], we train all
methods on SAM3 using the YTVOS18 training set [50] for 10 epochs. For a fair comparison, we
sample 8 frames per sequence and use the same learning-rate schedule across all methods. Training
is conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 48 GB of VRAM.
4.2
Evaluation Metrics
Following prior works [60, 24, 61], we use the standard VOS metrics [38] to evaluate adversarial
performance. Furthermore, while these metrics reflect overall attack effectiveness, they fail to
characterize the temporal dynamics of an attack, such as how quickly the attack takes effect or how
long it could persist. To capture these aspects, we further introduce three temporal metrics: Latency
(Lat), Coverage (Cov), and Delayed-Coverage (Covk).
Let Fv denote the set of valid frames successfully tracked by the benign VOS model. Specifically, a
frame is considered valid if its benign score (e.g. J &F) exceeds 0.3, following [60]. Among these
valid frames, we define the set of collapsed frames Fc as those whose score under attack drops by at
least 50% relative to the benign score.
Based on these two sets, we define: • Latency (Lat), which measures the normalized temporal
position of the first collapsed frame in Fv and thus reflects how quickly the attack takes effect;
• Coverage (Cov), which measures the proportion of collapsed frames in Fv and thus reflects the
temporal persistence of the attack; and • Delayed-Coverage (Covk), which measures the attack
coverage Cov for the mid-video attack starting from frame k. Formally, these metrics are defined as:

foreground and background features, thus collapsing predictions across all prompt modalities.

multiple VOS benchmarks. For each video, we compute pairwise cross-prompt cosine similarities
separately for decoder and memory features using mask, point, and box prompts. As reported in

consistent regardless of whatever prompts. This reveals the basis of AUV’s prompt-agnosticism,

5
Limitations and Broader Impact
AUV assumes white-box access to the target VOS model during offline UAP optimization, which
is consistent with prior works that require gradient-based source-model access [22, 57, 60], and
therefore does not currently support black-box optimization. Once optimized, the fixed UAP requires
no further model access, prompt, or per-video optimization. Meanwhile, AUV is primarily designed
for memory-based VOS and may be less effective on earlier architectures without explicit memory
mechanisms. The attack could potentially be misused to disrupt deployed VOS systems; we therefore
emphasize its intended use only for robustness evaluation and scientific research. It can also serve
as a tool for assessing VOS robustness to adversarial perturbations and guiding the development of
more resilient models.
6
Conclusion
We propose AUV, a universal adversarial attack framework for VOS models. AUV aligns its
optimization with the two-stage VOS inference paradigm by decoupling UAP training into target
initialization and memory propagation stages with tailored supervisions. By corrupting the shared
memory space, it achieves strong prompt-agnostic attacks across masks, points, boxes, and language.
Extensive experiments demonstrate AUV’s state-of-the-art attack performance and the framework’s
broad applicability across VOS models. It delivers the fastest attack onset and superior persistence,
while supporting flexible mid-video attacks, providing a strong baseline for future research.

Full Results
Due to space constraints in the main paper, we provide full comparisons of different attack methods on SAM3
[2] across prompt modalities and benchmarks here. For a fair comparison, all baselines use the same training
configurations as ours, as described in Section 4.1.
As shown in Table 10, AUV consistently surpasses existing attack baselines across all VOS benchmarks and
prompt types, including mask, point, and box prompts. For example, on DAVIS17-val with point prompts,
AUV achieves 7.1 J &F, outperforming the previous SOTA UAP-SAM2 [60] of 23.9, even though the latter is
specifically optimized for point-based attacks. On more challenging benchmarks such as MOSEv2 and MOSEv1,
AUV also achieves the best performance across all prompt types. Notably, on MOSEv1 with mask prompts,
AUV outperforms previous SOTA by 15.3 J &F.
Also, as discussed in Section 4.2 of the main paper, standard VOS metrics do not capture the temporal dynamics
of an attack. Therefore, we further compare AUV with the baselines in terms of attack onset (Lat), persistence
(Cov), and mid-video persistence (Covk). As shown in Table 11, AUV demonstrates a clear advantage across
all temporal metrics: it takes effect much faster, achieves long-lasting suppression, and exhibits robust online
attack capability, driven by its propagation-aligned training on memory-based propagation.
For language-based segmentation, AUV achieves the strongest attack performance across all three subsets of
SA-Co/VEval, as shown in Table 12. Notably, it reduces cgF1 and pHOTA on YT-Temporal-1B to 0.3 and 9.1,
respectively, significantly surpassing the previous SOTA of UAP-SAM2 (3.5 and 26.2, respectively). We further

Algorithm
Algorithm 1 summarizes the optimization process of AUV, which aligns with the two-stage inference paradigm
of modern VOS models through a two-phase curriculum.
In Phase I, we optimize the UAP δ to corrupt the initial memory anchor ˜E1 before it is permanently stored in the
memory bank. In Phase II, we jointly optimize the same δ to corrupt the initial memory anchor and flatten the
spatial saliency of memory-attended feature maps ˜Ai, disrupting both initialization and propagation stages.

then evaluate different SAM2 extensions, including SAM2Long [19], SAMURAI [51], and DAM4SAM [47],
which incorporate their respective memory-management strategies. As shown in Table 13, AUV remains highly
effective across different memory-level defenses.

E.1
Prompt Variations
We adopt the standard practice for evaluating point and box prompts in the main paper, following UAP-SAM2
[60] and DarkSAM [61], which uses the center point and tight bounding box derived from the target mask,
respectively. To further evaluate the robustness of AUV, we test if prompt variations can successfully bypass the
perturbation. Specifically, we evaluate (1) random shifts in point positions, (2) variations in point count, and (3)
random translation and scaling of bounding boxes.
As shown in Table 14, AUV maintains consistent attack performance across prompt variations, demonstrating
strong robustness to changes in prompt position, translation, and count. These results further indicate that
AUV generalizes across diverse prompt patterns rather than relying on a specific prompt distribution.
