Abstract
Surgical video analysis requires video object segmentation
(VOS), yet existing methods are commonly based on pre-
trained model fine-tuning using a large number of expert
labels. In-context segmentation (ICS) offers the promise of
bypassing this high cost by segmenting a target video us-
ing a few annotated references, which are the sole source
of surgical knowledge for a frozen model. However, exist-
ing ICS methods tend to draw such references at random,
which concentrate on a few views that dominate a surgical
video and can miss other objects of interest (e.g., transient
instruments and small structures). To address these chal-
lenges, we propose INCISE, a training-free ICS framework
for surgical videos. INCISE selects unlabeled frames from
multiple surgical videos that jointly cover the local appear-
ances of a target video, and then segments the target video
using these references. With only 1.7% of training frames
selected and annotated as references and no target video
frame labeled, INCISE obtains 67.2% mean mIoU and out-
performs the best SOTA fine-tuned method by 9.2 points.
We hope INCISE can help establish ICS as a practical al-
ternative to fine-tuning in surgical video analysis and other
domains with a costly labeling burden.
1. Introduction
Segmentation of instruments and anatomy in surgical
videos supports both intraoperative guidance and surgical
education. Models trained on such masks delineate safe and
dangerous dissection zones during an operation [24, 32].
They also support automatic assessment of the critical view
of safety in laparoscopic cholecystectomy [26]. Such mod-
els further help teach surgery [19, 51]: medical students
learned from a trained model to recognize anatomical land-
marks nearly as well as from a surgeon tutor [30].
Yet most existing methods fine-tune pretrained models
on thousands of pixel-level masks, which surgeons hardly
have time to draw [25]. To scale up annotation, clinicians
typically annotate a few representative frames as visual
guidelines and train non-expert annotators such as medical
students to label the remaining frames [6, 13, 42]. Even so,
a medical student still needs about 25 minutes to annotate a
single frame [39], so curating a training set remains a major

bottleneck. Fine-tuned video methods built on vision foun-
dation models (VFMs) inherit this cost [23, 38]: they adapt
a VFM on such labeled frames and further require an expert
to label at least one frame of each target video (Fig. 2a).
In parallel, semi-supervised video object segmentation
(Semi-VOS) [5, 36] removes the fine-tuning by keeping a
VFM frozen, yet still needs expert masks on one or more
frames of every target video (Fig. 2b).
Such masks are
rarely available in practice, because an expert must anno-
tate each new video before it can be segmented. Even then,
the labeled frames can miss classes that appear only else-
where in the video, and choosing which frames to label is
itself an open problem [10, 14, 55].
In-context segmentation (ICS) removes both costs: a
frozen VFM segments a target video from a few annotated
images of other videos (called references) given at test time,
with neither fine-tuning nor a target-video mask [9, 21, 50,
58]. Just as medical students learn what to segment from
a few surgeon-labeled examples, an ICS model takes such
references as context and segments the target surgical video
itself (Fig. 1c; Fig. 2c). If a few annotated surgical frames
could stand in for a training set, clinicians would need to
annotate only these frames. However, because these ref-
erences are the sole source of surgical knowledge for the
frozen VFM, existing ICS methods [44, 58] simply draw
them at random [28].
Choosing references at random may not work well since
surgical appearances vary widely in duration and scale.
Specifically, major tissues and routine tools may remain vis-
ible in many frames, whereas a specialized instrument may
enter the view only briefly [46] and a delicate structure may
occupy only a small region [6, 13, 33]. Consequently, a
handful of randomly drawn frames mostly capture frequent
appearances [18] and miss these brief or small classes al-
together, thus leaving a frozen VFM with no reference to
segment them. Hence, we study whether a small budget of
well-chosen references from other surgical videos can give
a frozen VFM enough coverage to segment a target video.
To this end, we introduce INCISE, a training-free ap-
proach that augments frozen VFMs with references selected
for each target video (Fig. 1a,b). It operates in two stages.
(i) Coverage-based selection picks a few unlabeled frames
from other videos that jointly cover the cluster centers of the
target video with DINOv3 [41] local features. It weights
the centers of rare clusters more, so brief instruments and
small structures can also receive references. (ii) Segmenta-
tion with independent pseudo-labels reads the video frame
by frame with DINOv3 and SAM 3 and labels each frame
with these references. Its memory keeps independent per-
frame pseudo-labels instead of its own predictions, so errors
do not accumulate. We make three contributions:
• We identify a core limitation of existing surgical video
segmentation paradigms: supervised methods demand
costly domain or per-video annotations, whereas training-
free in-context approaches fail under complicated spatio-
temporal distributions.
• We propose INCISE, a training-free framework coupling
coverage-based reference selection over weighted-rare lo-
cal clusters with a history of independent pseudo-labels
that prevents temporal error accumulation in frozen foun-
dation models.
• Extensive evaluations across 6 surgical benchmarks and
cross-domain transfers show that INCISE surpasses fine-
tuned paradigms and that strategic visual coverage out-
weighs raw annotation volume for robust generalization.
2. Related Work
Surgical video segmentation.
Surgical scene segmen-
tation is benchmarked on laparoscopic, robotic and micro-
scopic videos with pixel-level masks [3, 13, 17, 33]. Super-
vised models such as ISINet learn instrument classes from
many annotated frames [12]. Recent methods adapt foun-
dation models to medical and surgical video.
MedSAM
and SurgicalSAM adapt SAM to medical and surgical im-
ages with mask supervision [22, 57]. MedSAM2 fine-tunes
SAM 2.1 on large collections of annotated 3D medical im-
ages and videos [23]. D-GEM trains a decoder on DINOv3
features and stores annotated frames of each target video in
a dual memory [38]. Both need masks drawn on frames of
every target video, while INCISE trains nothing and uses no
mask of the target video. Closest to our setting, Mousavi et
al. [28] start SAM 2.1 in a cholecystectomy video from an
annotated frame of another patient. They draw this frame at

In-context segmentation.
In-context segmentation mod-
els segment a target image from a few annotated refer-
ences. Generalist models learn this ability from segmenta-
tion data. Painter and SegGPT train one model on mixtures
of dense prediction and segmentation datasets [49, 50], and
COSINE trains a decoder on frozen DINOv2 and CLIP fea-
tures [21]. UniverSeg and Tyche train such models on med-
ical images [4, 35]. Training-free methods instead match
the features of frozen VFMs. PerSAM turns matches be-
tween the references and the target into point prompts for
SAM [58].
INSID3 segments with a single frozen DI-
NOv3 backbone [9, 41], and RSRM applies such matching
to cross-domain few-shot segmentation [44]. All of these
methods take the references as given. On video, SegGPT,
COSINE and PerSAM are prompted with annotations of the
target video [21, 50, 58]. DC-SAM prompts SAM 2 from
one external reference instead, but it trains a prompt gener-
ator and parts of SAM 2 for this task [34].
Reference selection.
Video object segmentation meth-
ods choose which frame of the target video to annotate [10,
14, 55], so every new video needs annotations that no other
video can reuse. Active learning selects samples to annotate
for training a model [20, 40, 60], and with few labels repre-
sentative samples work better than uncertain ones [15]. For
a nearest-neighbor classifier, selecting training data reduces
to maximizing a submodular facility-location function [52].
Visual in-context learning usually retrieves references for
each target image from a fully annotated pool [48, 59].
Vote-k, SCS and MVPS are closest to ours because they
select from an unlabeled pool [43, 45, 53]. They work on
text or single images, and SCS and MVPS train a selection
agent or retriever on labeled data. INCISE trains nothing
and picks unlabeled frames of other videos that jointly cover
the weighted-rare cluster centers of each target video.
Memory in video segmentation.
Memory-based video
object segmentation reads past frames together with their
predicted masks [8, 31]. SAM 2 propagates masks through
a memory of recent frames encoded with its own predicted
masks [36], and the SAM 3 tracker inherits this design [5].
An erroneous mask in this memory is read again on later
frames, so errors accumulate [11, 54]. Later trackers filter
what enters the memory by confidence, motion or distractor
cues [5, 11, 47, 54]. D-GEM likewise gates its predictions
by confidence before writing them to memory [38]. In all
of them, outputs of the tracker itself still enter the memory.
Our history stores only pseudo-labels computed afresh from
the references and the current frame, so no prediction is read
back by later frames.

3. Method
Our goal is to segment a target surgical video with B anno-
tated references alone, using frozen VFMs without training.
The references come from other videos of the same dataset
and are chosen before any of their masks is revealed. Sur-
gical masks cost expert time, so only the chosen frames are
ever annotated. A key property of DINOv3 is the strong
local self-similarity of its dense features [9]. We use it to
find suitable references: the local appearances of the tar-
get video group into clusters, and we select the pool frames
that jointly cover their centers. To keep errors from accu-
mulating in memory, we derive pseudo-labels from dense
correspondences between each frame and these references.
A frozen SAM 3 tracker stores these pseudo-labels in its
memory instead of its own predictions.
Our approach, named INCISE and illustrated in Fig. 3,
first selects references from images alone so that they cover
the local appearances of the target video (Sec. 3.1). It then
segments the video frame by frame from these references
(Sec. 3.2). Frozen DINOv3 features transfer labels, and a
frozen SAM 3 tracker reads them with past pseudo-labels.

3.2. Segmentation with independent pseudo-labels
The segmenter combines two complementary sources. La-
bel transfer matches every patch of a frame against the ref-
erences, so it finds each class wherever its appearance oc-
curs but gives coarse boundaries. SAM 3 memory attention
gives sharp and temporally consistent masks, but it can only
follow classes held in its memory. We combine the two so
that each covers the weakness of the other.

4. Experiments
We evaluate INCISE on six surgical benchmarks against
methods that receive a ground-truth mask of each target
video. We also compare with methods that, like ours, re-
ceive no mask of the target video and segment it from an-
notated references of other surgeries. We report these re-
sults and the transfer to polyp segmentation in Sec. 4.2. We
then analyze how references are chosen and how many are
needed (Sec. 4.3), and we ablate the segmenter (Sec. 4.4).
4.1. Experimental setup
Datasets.
We evaluate on six multi-class surgical bench-
marks from three specialties:
general surgery (Cholec-
Seg8k [17], SISVSE [56] and DSAD [6]), urology (En-
doVis 2018 [3] and SAR-RARP50 [33]) and ophthalmol-
ogy (CaDIS [13]).
DSAD labels only organs and SAR-
RARP50 only tools and suture material; the others label
both anatomy and instruments. Each training split forms
the reference pool, and each test video is a target video
(supplementary material). PolypGen [1] tests transfer to
colonoscopy.
Metrics.
We report mIoU including background, except
on CaDIS, which has no background class. Class IoUs are
averaged within each frame, omitting classes absent from
both prediction and ground truth. Frames are then aver-
aged per target video, target videos per dataset, and the six
datasets equally for the mean. On PolypGen we report the
frame-averaged Dice and IoU defined in their paper [1].
Implementation and protocol.
INCISE uses DINOv3
ViT-L/16 [41] and the SAM 3 tracker [5], both frozen. Se-
lection uses K = 128 centers, β = 0.5 and B = 10 ref-
erences per target video, and the segmenter keeps H = 12
history frames. One configuration serves every dataset.

4.2. Main results
Baselines.
Table 1 compares INCISE with state-of-the-
art surgical, medical and in-context segmentation meth-
ods in mIoU on the six surgical benchmarks. We group
the baselines by whether they train and whether they are
given a mask of the target video.
The primary base-
lines are training-free ICS methods such as PerSAM [58],
RSRM [44] and INSID3 [9], which share our setting. They
receive no mask of the target video and segment it from ref-
erences of other surgeries drawn at random. For RSRM and
INSID3, we also report variants that receive the reference
sets selected by INCISE. We also report methods that re-
ceive a ground-truth mask of each target video, such as the
training-free trackers SAM 2 [36] and SAM 3 [5]. Fine-
tuned methods such as MedSAM2 [23], SegGPT [50], CO-
SINE [21] and D-GEM [38] take the same mask as input
and also train with mask supervision. These methods need
an expert to annotate every target video, so they operate un-
der a stronger supervision regime than ours. INCISE trains
nothing and uses no mask of the target video, and it alone
selects its references to cover that video.
Surgical video segmentation.
INCISE achieves the
best result on all six benchmarks without training or any
mask of the target video (Tab. 1). Its mean of 67.2% is
9.2 points above the strongest fine-tuned method and 14.7
points above SAM 3 prompted with a mask of the target
video. INCISE leads in each specialty, by 5.4 points in
general surgery, 6.9 in urology and 6.1 in ophthalmology.
Figure 5 shows qualitative results.
Methods given a mask of the target video, such as Seg-
GPT and COSINE, can follow only the classes in that mask.
Classes that appear later are common in surgery: across the
six benchmarks, 117 of the 370 classes of the target videos
are absent from the given mask. The references of INCISE
stay in memory for the whole video, so a class that enters
late can still be matched.
Training-free ICS methods share our setting, yet INCISE
outperforms them by 26.9 points or more. Their references
come from random draws or similarity retrieval (Sec. 4.3),
and they segment each frame independently, without tem-
poral information (Sec. 4.4). However, our selection helps
RSRM and INSID3 (7.2 and 2.6 points in mean mIoU), as
the selected references cover the appearances of the target
video. In contrast, INCISE also uses temporal information
through its pseudo-label history and stays 24.3 points ahead
of both with the same references. Across the six reference
pools, INCISE annotates 1.7% of the pool frames (supple-
mentary material). Of its reference slots, 18.9% reuse a

4.3. On the choice of references
Since the references are the only source of surgical knowl-
edge for the frozen models, we ask which frames should be
annotated and how many are needed.
Coverage versus similarity.
We first compare cover-
age with random references and with similarity retrieval in
Tab. 3. Similarity retrieval is the usual choice in visual in-
context learning [48, 59]: it ranks pool frames by the co-
sine similarity of their global DINOv3 feature to the mean
feature of the target video, which favors its dominant view.
Coverage improves every dataset with both segmenters, in-
dicating that the gain originates in the references rather
than in SAM 3. With the full INCISE it adds 5.9 points
over similarity and 10.5 over random references, and with
dense label transfer alone it adds 5.6 and 9.6. As shown
in Fig. 6b, performance stays stable across the balancing
strength β, since clustering local features already enforces
broad visual diversity; setting β = 0.5 yields the best over-
all mean (70.3%) and improves worst-frame coverage and
tool-heavy benchmarks such as SAR-RARP50 (+0.5 points
over β = 1, supplementary material).
Annotation budget and cost.
Finally, we study the num-
ber of references per target video and its cost in Fig. 6c and d
(per dataset in the supplementary material). Accuracy rises
steeply up to about ten references and then saturates, in-
dicating that beyond a small budget the choice of frames
matters more than their number. Going from one to ten
references raises the mean by 8.6 points, while more refer-
ences add at most 0.17. The coverage objective explains this
saturation: it is submodular, so each added reference con-
tributes less new coverage of the target video than the one
before. Notably, even at B = 1—matching the single-mask
budget of target-guided baselines—INCISE reaches 60.7%
mean mIoU (supplementary material), already surpassing
SAM 3 [5] (55.9%) and SegGPT [50] (48.6%) prompted
with the ground-truth first-frame mask of the target video.
We also give the target-guided baselines the masks of the
first ten frames of each target video, close to the 8.1 pool
frames that INCISE annotates per target video on average.
INCISE still exceeds the mean mIoU of SAM 3 by 6.6
points and of SAM 2 by 7.7 points (supplementary mate-
rial). Its margins over D-GEM and MedSAM2 are 12.6 and
25.9 points. From 10 to 100 references, peak GPU memory
rises from 7.46 to 33.90 GB and per-frame time from 0.95
to 2.54 s. We therefore use ten references per target video.

4.5. Cross-dataset transfer
Finally, we test whether INCISE can segment a target video

synthetic to real images (Synth-Colon [27]).
Each pool
holds all eligible source images, and we evaluate on the
classes shared with the target.
Coverage gives the best mIoU in all four pairs, with
the largest gain of 7.66 points over the stronger baseline
on Endoscapes →CholecSeg8k.
Under the appearance
shifts of EndoVis 2017 →2018 and Synth-Colon →Polyp-
Gen C6, similarity retrieval even falls below random selec-
tion. Matching the mean target feature then selects redun-
dant source frames of a single visual mode, which coverage
avoids by covering the whole target video. These results
show that INCISE can reuse existing annotated datasets, in-
cluding synthetic renders, for new surgical procedures and
clinical centers without target-domain annotations.
8


5. Conclusion
We showed that in-context segmentation can be an
alternative to fine-tuning for surgical video once its
references are chosen with care.
INCISE selects a few
frames of other surgeries that jointly cover the local
appearances of each target video. A frozen tracker then
reads these references with a history of independent
pseudo-labels, so errors do not accumulate.
Without
training or any mask of the target video, it outperforms
methods fine-tuned on thousands of surgical frames.
Beyond a small budget, which frames are annotated matters
more than how many.
We hope this work makes ICS
practical in surgery and other domains with costly labels.
