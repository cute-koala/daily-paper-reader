Abstract
Feed-forward 4D trackers recover camera motion, depth,
and point trajectories from short video clips, but chaining
windows over long videos degrades their accuracy. We find
that replacing inter-window alignment with ground-truth
transforms leaves tracking error essentially unchanged.
Static-point error instead grows with time since last obser-
vation: repeatedly predicting a static point from windows
that no longer observe it corrupts previously recovered ge-
ometry. We call this failure forgetting. Merely retaining the
last observed positions of unseen points reduces error by
26% on PointOdyssey without modifying the tracker. We in-
troduce Recall4D, a training-free framework that augments
a frozen tracker with a persistent world state and sparse
motion anchors. Static points retain their world-space po-
sitions outside the camera view and are refined by averag-
ing observations decoded at sparse keyframes while in view.
For dynamic objects, the tracker decodes sparse represen-
tative anchors, while geometric skinning propagates their
displacements to dense queries. Across two long-horizon
benchmarks, Recall4D improves the accuracy of its back-
bone while running 7.6–9.0× faster, and its accuracy gains
carry over to five further backbones and three other long-
sequence tasks, demonstrating its generality. Project page:
https://kincin.github.io/Recall4D/.
1. Introduction
Feed-forward models have changed how we recover 3D mo-
tion from video [35, 39, 46]. Given a short clip, a single
network pass now predicts camera poses, depth, and the
3D trajectories of query points, without per-video optimiza-
tion [19, 23, 43]. Real videos, however, are long. To process
them, these models slide a window over the video and chain
the windows: each window is aligned to the previous one,
and every tracked point is handed over as a 3D position and
a local descriptor [11, 14, 17]. This works for a few hundred
frames. Then it breaks down: as Fig. 1(b) shows, the sur-
vival rate of Point4D drops sharply as videos grow longer.
The natural explanation is accumulated drift across win-

alignment or loop closure [5, 26, 30]. However, our anal-
ysis in Sec. 3 reveals a different dominant failure: forget-

alignment with ground truth leaves the error unchanged. In-
stead, the error grows with how long a point has been out of
view. Once a point leaves the view, the stateless tracker
repeatedly re-predicts its position from windows that no
longer observe it, causing even static points to wander over
time, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Simply retaining their last ob-
served positions reduces this error by 26% without mod-
ifying the model. These findings suggest that the key to
long-range tracking is not better alignment, but a persis-
tent memory of previously observed geometry [34, 38, 41].
This failure also exposes a deeper inefficiency: the tracker
spends much of its computation predicting what it has al-
ready seen. Static points are decoded even after hundreds
of frames out of view, although they never move.
For moving objects, the redundancy is equally funda-
mental: their points move, but not independently. Their
trajectories are coupled by shared object motion [17, 37],
yet the tracker predicts each point as if its motion were inde-
pendent. If points on the same object move together, why de-
code every one of them? A few representative anchors can
capture the shared motion and propagate it to the rest. The
problem is therefore twofold: re-predicting what should be
remembered, and decoding what can be inferred.
This diagnosis calls for a different fix: not a better align-
ment, but a memory; not more queries, but fewer. We intro-
duce Recall4D, which equips a frozen feed-forward tracker
with a persistent world state and sparse motion anchors.
Static background points are written into the state in world
coordinates. Whether a stored point lies within the camera
view is decided by projecting the state, not the latest pre-
diction, into the predicted camera. The state is refined from
sparse keyframe observations and preserved when a point is
out of view. At every frame, the state, rather than a fresh
prediction, is the output. Moving objects are handled dif-
ferently. A small set of anchors is tracked by the decoder,
while the remaining points follow their motion through ge-
ometric skinning. The result is a stateful tracker that re-
members static geometry and propagates dynamic motion
without decoding every point at every frame.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
• We diagnose forgetting of previously observed geome-
try, rather than inter-window registration drift, as a ma-
jor cause of long-horizon degradation in feed-forward 4D
reconstruction, supported by a fine-grained error analysis.
• We propose Recall4D, a training-free framework that
equips frozen feed-forward 4D trackers with a persis-
tent world state for static geometry and sparse motion
anchors for dynamic objects, avoiding redundant predic-
tions through geometric skinning.

2. Related Work
Long-range 2D and 3D point tracking.
TAP-Vid [6]
established point tracking as a benchmark task, followed
by window-based 2D trackers [7, 8, 11, 17, 18].
Spa-

and DELTA [28] extend tracking to 3D with depth, cam-

marks [16, 20, 47] enable systematic evaluation. Concur-
rently, TrackEverything [13] achieves long-horizon track-
ing through voxel-based deduplication and classification of
points as static or dynamic. We share this split but, instead
of building a new tracker, diagnose why existing trackers
fail over long videos and repair them without training.
Feed-forward 3D and 4D reconstruction. Feed-forward
reconstruction has advanced from pairwise pointmap pre-
diction [21, 39] to multi-view geometry estimation [35, 40]
and dynamic reconstruction [46]. Recent methods directly
predict 4D geometry and point trajectories [9, 19, 23–
25, 31, 45], including our backbone Point4D [14]. Extend-
ing such models to long videos requires chaining overlap-
ping windows through alignment, sometimes with loop clo-
sure [5]. These pipelines treat alignment as the bottleneck;
we show that, for point tracking, re-predicting previously
observed geometry matters more.

SLAM [26, 30] maintains persistent maps.

3. Diagnosing Long-Horizon Degradation
Why do feed-forward 4D trackers fail as videos grow
longer? The natural explanation is accumulated drift: small
misalignments at each window handover compound and
corrupt the reconstruction. We first pursued this hypothe-
sis by improving cross-window alignment and smoothing
predictions over the overlapping frames, but neither helped,
as detailed in Supp. A. The problem therefore seems to lie
not in how windows are connected, but in how point esti-
mates are carried across them. To find out, we evaluate the
chained baseline on the 1,000-frame PointOdyssey [47] val-
idation set. Tab. 1 breaks the error down by point class, and
Fig. 3 shows how it evolves over time.
Is alignment drift really the culprit? At each window
handover, the baseline estimates a Sim(3) from the pre-
dicted depth of the overlapping frames.
Relative to the
ground-truth camera poses, these estimates are off by 1.2◦
in rotation and 3.4% in scale on average. In Tab. 1(a), we
replace every one of them with the transform implied by
the ground-truth poses, so that the chain carries no registra-
tion error. If accumulated misalignment drove the degrada-
tion, this oracle would recover much of the lost accuracy. It
does not. EPE stays at 1.568 against 1.569 for the baseline,
and the error curve in Fig. 3(a) is indistinguishable from the
original over the whole video. Whatever degrades Point4D
over time, it is not how its windows are registered.
A static point does not move. Its prediction does. In
Fig. 3(b), we therefore plot the error against the time since
a point was last observed.
For static points, EPE rises
from 1.25 while visible to 2.30 after more than 400 un-
seen frames, and with perfect registration it still reaches
2.45.
The ground-truth position of a static point never
changes; what changes is the tracker’s estimate. Once the
point leaves the view, Point4D keeps decoding its position
from windows that contain no observation of it. Each new
estimate replaces the previous one and is handed to the
next window, progressively corrupting geometry that was
already recovered. The same holds, to a lesser degree, for
points in view: every window re-derives a point from the
single 3D position handed over to it, and nothing ties the
new estimate to the earlier ones. The EPE of visible static
points thus doubles from 0.83 in the first 200 frames to 1.71
in the last 200, although the share of unseen points stays
near 50% after the first window. The tracker is not losing
the scene because the scene moves or because its windows
drift apart, but because it keeps re-predicting what it should
have remembered. We call this failure forgetting.
What if we simply stop re-predicting? The diagnosis sug-
gests a direct test. Without modifying the network, improv-
ing alignment, or adding a learned memory, we change one
rule: once a point has been unseen for a whole window,
it keeps its last observed position until it is seen again. In
Tab. 1(b), visibility is decided by Point4D’s own cameras,
which detect points leaving the frame but not occlusions.
This rule alone reduces EPE by 26% and raises SR from
0.193 to 0.260. With ground-truth visibility in Tab. 1(d),
the reduction reaches 33%. Adding GT-pose alignment on
top of the hold in Tab. 1(c) changes nothing. Remembering
observed positions thus removes a quarter to a third of the
error, whereas perfect registration removes none. Forget-
ting, not registration drift, drives long-horizon degradation.
Not all points need to be treated alike. Tab. 1 reveals a
second insight. With ground-truth visibility, holding cuts
the out-of-frame EPE of static points by 57%, but the EPE

Comparison Methods. We compare Recall4D with two
methods designed for long videos, Point4D [14] and the
concurrent TrackEverything [13] in its sparse (s) and dense

SpatialTrackerV2 [43] and the feed-forward 4D recon-
struction methods V-DPM [31], 4RC [25], Any4D [19],
TraceAnything [23], and Track4World [24].
Following
Point4D, SpatialTrackerV2 uses its sliding-window infer-
ence, and the feed-forward methods are chained with its se-
lection strategy, which projects each point into every over-
lapping frame and re-queries it at the frame whose projected
depth best matches the predicted depth.
Datasets.
We evaluate Recall4D on two long-sequence
benchmarks: PointOdyssey [47], containing videos with an
average length of 2,035 frames, and Dynamic Replica [16],
featuring 900-frame test sequences. Both provide dynamic
scenes with complex motion, occlusions, and long point tra-
jectories. Diagnosis and ablations use the validation splits,
and all comparisons use the test splits.
Metrics. We report End-Point Error (EPE); the Average
Percentage of points within Distance thresholds (APD), av-
eraged over δ3D ∈{0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 1.0} m; Survival Rate
(SR) [14], the average fraction of the video a point is tracked
before its error first exceeds δ3D, averaged over the same
thresholds; EPEdyn, the EPE of moving queries, whose
ground truth departs by more than 0.05 from its first-frame
position; and the average inference time per video in sec-
onds (Time). Errors are computed in first-frame camera co-
ordinates after one global median scale per video.
6. Results and Discussion
6.1. Long-Horizon 4D Tracking
Quantitative comparison. Tab. 2 compares Recall4D with
3D trackers and feed-forward 4D methods.
Recall4D is
fastest in all settings, running 7.8–9.0× faster than [14]. At
500 frames, it achieves the best EPE, APD, and SR on both
datasets. At 200 frames on PointOdyssey, [43] achieves
lower EPE and [13] higher APD and SR, but both are 6–
20× slower. From 200 to 500 frames, Recall4D’s EPE rises
from 0.609 to 0.836, versus 0.492 to 1.037 for [43], showing
better robustness as video length grows.
Long-horizon scalability. As Tab. 3 shows, Recall4D low-
ers the EPE of Point4D by 12–14%, doubles its SR at 2,000
frames, and runs 7.6× faster. On TrackEverything, it im-
proves every metric at every length with 14–16% less run-
time, but by smaller margins, since the concurrent Track-
Everything also keeps static points in place. This indepen-
dent convergence supports our diagnosis that forgetting lim-
its long-horizon reconstruction. Absolute errors neverthe-
less remain high for every method beyond 1,500 frames, a
residual shared by all backbones that a training-free plug-in
cannot correct; Recall4D removes the forgetting component
at a fraction of the cost. We further discuss TrackEverything
in detail in Supp. D.1.

6.3. Accuracy-Efficiency Analysis
Fig. 6(c, d) scales the video length and the number of
queries. From 200 to 2,000 frames, Recall4D is 7.6–7.8×
faster than Point4D, taking 264 s instead of 2,019 s at 2,000
frames, and attains a lower EPE at every length. As the
number of queries grows from 256 to 32,768, the runtime
of Point4D increases 46.8×, whereas that of Recall4D in-
creases only 3.4×, since added queries are either stored or
skinned rather than decoded at every frame. The speed-up
thus rises from 7.9× at 8,192 queries to 13.2× at 32,768,
with a lower EPE at every query budget.
6.4. Generalization Across Backbones and Tasks
Cross-backbone generalization. To verify that Recall4D’s
effectiveness is not tied to a specific backbone, we fur-
ther evaluate its generalization across different feed-forward
4D trackers. Recall4D requires no training and leaves the
backbone unchanged, so it applies to other window-chained
feed-forward models. Tab. 6 applies it to five further back-
bones [19, 23–25, 31], each chained as in Point4D [14]. Re-
call4D improves every metric on every backbone, reducing
EPE by 10–23% and doubling SR on TraceAnything, while
also reducing runtime.

7. Conclusion
Feed-forward 4D trackers degrade on long videos, and
we traced this degradation to forgetting rather than inter-
window misalignment: ground-truth alignment leaves the
error unchanged, whereas simply retaining unseen points
reduces it by 26%. Building on this diagnosis, we intro-
duced Recall4D, a training-free framework that equips a
frozen tracker with a persistent world state for static ge-
ometry and sparse motion anchors for dynamic objects.
Recall4D improves long-horizon accuracy while tracking
2,000 frames 7.6× faster, and the same principle carries
over to other backbones, a concurrent long-video tracker,
and three further tasks.
Moving objects still depend on
the backbone’s motion prediction, and extending memory
to them is a natural next step. We hope these findings en-
courage long-video 4D reconstruction to treat memory, and
not only alignment, as a core design problem.

A. Additional Details on Error Analysis
This section details the analysis of Sec. 3: the point classes
behind Tab. 1 and Fig. 3, the treatment of scene-exiting
ground-truth tracks, and how each intervention is imple-
mented. Every intervention is applied to Point4D’s own
output, with the released weights unchanged.
Point classes. The analysis uses the queries and metrics of
Sec. 5. Each (query, frame) pair is assigned one of three
ground-truth states: visible, occluded, meaning in front of
the camera and inside the image but hidden, or out of frame.
Together with the static or dynamic label of the query, this
gives the six classes of Tab. 1. The EPE of a class is com-
puted per video over the pairs of that class, then averaged
over the videos with at least 100 such pairs. The EPE over
all points is averaged over videos in the same way, so it is
not the share-weighted mean of the class values.
Scene-exiting ground-truth tracks. Some PointOdyssey
tracks leave the scene: their ground-truth positions jump to
hundreds or thousands of meters from the rest of the scene.
Let r be the median distance of a video’s valid ground-
truth positions to their median point. A track is marked as
scene-exiting if a valid position lies farther than 25r from
that point, or if it moves by more than 5r between two con-
secutive valid frames. This removes 145 of 5,979 tracks in
ani14 new f and 462 of 6,642 tracks in r1 new f, all on
moving objects, and none elsewhere. The same tracks are
removed for every method. They do not affect any conclu-
sion but dominate the dynamic out-of-frame class: as Tab. 8
shows, under the standard protocol its EPE exceeds 14 for
every method, and the overall EPE of Point4D rises from
1.569 to 3.831.

dow k in the predicted camera frame of its first frame tk,

estimates a Sim(3) from the predicted depth of the overlap-

by the one implied by the ground-truth camera poses. With
Wt the ground-truth world-to-camera transform and sk the
scale of window k, measured as the median ratio between
predicted and ground-truth camera distances of the tracks
visible at tk, a point x in window k maps to

Holding unseen points. Given any tracker’s output, a point
that has been unseen for more than 40 consecutive frames,
the stride between windows, is output at its last seen posi-

Each task attaches the persistent world state of
Sec. 4.2 to an existing model, with the adaptations below.
Task A. CoTracker3 tracks in 2D, so the state is kept in
3D and projected back into the image. Points outside the
SAM 3.1 instances of the first frame are static, and are lifted
to 3D and stored as in Sec. 4.2. In every frame, the camera
is registered to the state by PnP with RANSAC on the 2D
positions from the tracker, with a 4-pixel threshold; if too
few inliers are found, the camera predicted by Point4D is
used. The state supplies the position of a static point only
while the tracker does not see it. A point that the registration
repeatedly rejects as an outlier is treated as a moving point
missed by the masks and returned to the tracker. Visibility
always comes from the tracker.
Task B. VGGT-Long [5] predicts a pointmap per chunk
and stacks the Sim(3)-aligned chunks, so a region is pre-
dicted again whenever a new chunk sees it.
We keep a
world-space state for static regions instead: observations
are fused by a running mean at keyframes, and a region
that leaves the view keeps its stored geometry. Vehicles and
pedestrians are removed with SAM 3.1 masks, and no an-
chors are needed since the benchmark evaluates the static
scene.
Task C. Video Depth Anything predicts depth indepen-
dently in each window, so the depth of a static region
changes between windows and upon revisits.
We back-
project static pixels into world coordinates with estimated
camera poses, fuse them into the state by a running mean
at keyframes, and read their depth back by projection. Dy-
namic pixels, given by SAM 3.1 masks, keep the original
prediction, so no anchors are needed.

This section reports three experiments: long-horizon results
without scene-exiting tracks, a closer look at the all-static
baseline, and an evaluation on real-world videos.
C.1. Results without Scene-Exiting Tracks
In Tab. 3, Recall4D lowers the EPE of Point4D by 12–14%
on the test split, against 43% on the validation videos of
Tab. 1. A large part of this difference comes from the eval-
uation protocol. Tab. 1 excludes scene-exiting ground-truth
tracks, whereas Tab. 3 follows the standard protocol and
keeps them. These tracks carry an error that is large and
nearly identical for every method, which dilutes any rela-
tive gain: as Tab. 8 shows, keeping them reduces the gain
on the validation videos from 43% to 18%.
We therefore re-evaluate Tab. 3 with the filter of Sec. A,
which removes 3.6% of the tracks. The predictions are un-
changed; only the set of evaluated tracks differs. As Tab. 10
shows, the EPE of Point4D at 2,000 frames falls from 5.334
to 1.598 and that of Recall4D from 4.634 to 0.924, so the
reduction brought by Recall4D rises from 13% to 42%. At
1,000 and 1,500 frames it is 31% and 40%, in line with
the 43% on the validation videos, and it grows with video
length, as expected if forgetting accumulates. SR is almost
unaffected by the filter: at 1,000 frames it is 0.282 and
0.410, against 0.282 and 0.408 in Tab. 3. Fewer than 4%
of the tracks thus account for most of the EPE under the
standard protocol, which is why we report bounded metrics
alongside it. With these tracks excluded, Point4D with Re-
call4D reaches the lowest EPE at every length, while Track-
Everything keeps the highest APD and SR. On TrackEvery-
thing, the gain from Recall4D remains small, as explained
in Sec. D.1.

C.3. Evaluation on Real-World Videos
Sec. 6.1 evaluates Recall4D on two synthetic datasets,
PointOdyssey [47] and Dynamic Replica [16].
The rea-
son is simple: our focus is long-horizon tracking, and exist-
ing real-world benchmarks with 3D track annotations con-
tain only short clips. Synthetic data alone, however, cannot
show whether our findings transfer to real videos. We there-
fore add an evaluation on the minival split of the Aria Digi-
tal Twin (ADT) [29] subset of TAPVid-3D [20]. Among the
three TAPVid-3D subsets, ADT has the longest clips, at 300
frames each, whereas DriveTrack [1] clips range from 25 to
300 frames and Panoptic Studio [15] clips have about 150.
ADT is also egocentric: frequent head motion moves scene
content out of view and back, which is exactly the condi-
tion under which forgetting occurs. At 300 frames, these
clips remain far shorter than our long-horizon setting, so
this experiment serves as a real-world validation rather than
a long-horizon benchmark. As Tab. 12 shows, our findings
transfer to real videos. Recall4D improves all six backbones
that have no built-in scene memory, on every metric: EPE
drops by 25% to 45%, SR rises by 0.06 to 0.23, and run-
time falls in every case, by up to 3.9×. These gains appear
on clips of only 300 frames, which suggests that forgetting
already matters at moderate length when the camera moves
as much as a head-mounted one.
TrackEverything [13] is the most accurate method on
this benchmark by a clear margin. On top of it, Recall4D
leaves accuracy essentially unchanged and reduces runtime
by 6%. This matches its behavior on PointOdyssey and has
the same explanation, given in Sec. D.1: TrackEverything
already keeps static points by design. The table thus tells
one story from two sides. The tracker that was built to re-
member is the most accurate, and the trackers that were not
move much closer to it once Recall4D lets them remember.
For 4RC, the gap to TrackEverything narrows by 72% in
EPE, 79% in APD, and 86% in SR, without any training.
D. Discussion and Limitations
D.1. A Closer Look at TrackEverything
Concurrent with our work, TrackEverything [13] made an
impressive step for long-horizon 3D tracking: a learned
tracker that follows every pixel through videos of more than
a thousand frames, built on a persistent, de-duplicated 3D
scene representation. As the work closest to ours, it de-
serves a closer look, which we give here. Tab. 13 compares
its design with ours. Both separate static from dynamic
points and spare the static ones from repeated trajectory pre-
diction. That an independently designed and trained tracker
arrives at the same decomposition is, in our view, strong
support for the diagnosis of Sec. 3. The two works are com-
plementary: TrackEverything learns this behavior inside a
new tracker, whereas Recall4D adds it to existing trackers
without training.

D.2. Why Not Try to Learn Something?
Recall4D involves no training. We reached this design only
after trying a range of alternatives, many of them learned,
and none brought a measurable gain. Tab. 15 lists them in
three groups: attempts to repair inter-window drift, attempts
to handle unseen points differently, and learned variants of
our components. They were run during development under
settings that differ from our final protocol, so each row is
compared only with its own control, and absolute values
are not comparable to the tables of the main paper.
Repairing inter-window drift. Our first hypothesis was
accumulated drift.
Detecting revisited places with a re-
identification head and correcting the chain upon a loop
leaves EPE at 1.839 against 1.820. Training the tracker on
chained windows, so that it sees its own handovers, changes
EPE by at most 1.3%, and passing a latent state from one
window to the next does not help either. Smoothing the
predicted trajectories over the overlapping frames, by lin-
ear interpolation across each overlap or by a 9-frame mov-
ing average, has no effect either: EPE stays between 1.821
and 1.823, and in the setting of Tab. 3 at 1,000 frames it
stays within 0.001 of 2.750 for Point4D and at 2.365 for
Recall4D. The windows already agree where they meet, so
smoothing their junctions cannot recover what was forgot-
ten. Together with the GT-pose alignment of Tab. 1, which
bounds what any alignment method can achieve, these re-
sults rule out registration as the main cause.
Handling unseen points. Holding helps static points but
not dynamic ones, so we tried to complete the motion of
unseen dynamic points: propagating them rigidly with their
object, interpolating between their last and next observa-
tions, and damping their predicted motion. None improves
on the control, and linear interpolation reaches only 1.349
even when both endpoints are taken from the ground truth.
Recall4D therefore leaves the motion of dynamic points to
the backbone and only reduces its cost. Deciding when to
hold also matters: gating the hold by the decoder’s predicted
visibility lowers EPE by 6.9%, against 22.3% with ground-
truth visibility. This gap led us to decide visibility by pro-
jecting the stored state into the predicted camera.
Learned variants. Fine-tuning the decoder jointly with our
modules degraded the backbone itself, from 0.961 to 1.006
on 200-frame videos, so we keep it frozen. A learned ro-
tation head for the anchors did not converge, its error stay-
ing at 14.4◦, which suggests that the frozen decoder fea-
tures carry little orientation information; our skinning there-
fore blends displacements only. Learned skinning weights
with a residual are no better than distance-based ones, 0.743
against 0.732. A learned retention gate separates static from
dynamic points better than a rule, with an AUC of 0.92
against 0.87, yet does not lower EPE. Finally, weighting ob-
servations by predicted confidence or by depth matches the
uniform mean, and confidence-guided updates raise EPE
from 1.378 to 1.427, since the predicted confidence is un-
correlated with the actual error. Even oracle weights would
gain only 2.3%, which is why we use a plain running mean.

D.4. Limitations and Outlook
Recall4D removes the forgetting of static geometry but in-
herits the motion prediction of its backbone, so it does not
improve how dynamic points themselves are tracked. Its
decomposition relies on first-frame instance masks, and a
moving object that these masks miss is treated as static. Ex-
tending memory to dynamic objects and making the decom-
position adaptive over time are natural next steps.
More broadly, a feed-forward model is stateless: what-
ever leaves its window must be inferred again. Our results
suggest that much of the long-horizon error is the price of
this statelessness and not of imperfect geometry, since a
small explicit state recovers a large part of it without any
training. The same observation holds across five further
backbones and three other tasks, and a concurrent tracker
arrives at it through learning. The open problem is mem-
ory for moving objects, where a stored position no longer
suffices and the state must also carry motion.
We also believe accuracy should not be the only axis on
which long-horizon 4D reconstruction is judged. A result
that arrives long after the video has ended is of limited use,
however accurate it is. Our experiments suggest that the
two goals need not be traded against each other: removing
repeated prediction lowers the error of Point4D on 1,000-
frame videos while cutting its runtime from 1,176 s to 155 s.
We see real-time long-horizon 4D reconstruction as the nat-
ural goal for the field, and we hope that future long-video
models will treat what they have already seen as something
to keep, and no longer as something to predict again.
