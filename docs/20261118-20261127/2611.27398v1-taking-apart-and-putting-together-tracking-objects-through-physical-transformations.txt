Abstract
We introduce Transformation Tracking and Understanding
(TTU), extending video object tracking to environments
where objects undergo physical assemblies and disassem-
blies. TTU requires continuously tracking objects through
transformations, identifying new entities, maintaining a state
graph of their relationships, and assigning semantic labels
to transformations. While foundation models (e.g., SAM 2/3)
maintain tracklets through appearance changes, they can-
not update semantic identities as objects physically evolve.
Prior work addresses only disassemblies, relying on ex-
pensive searches triggered only when tracking fails. We
propose Tracking Instances via Proposals and Overlaps
(TIPO), a training-free framework that continuously in-
fers transformations via a generate–evaluate–judge pipeline.
TIPO generates split and merge proposals from disjoint
mask components, scores them against mask-decoder priors
using inner and external overlap, and queries an MLLM
on the top proposal for visual verification and semantic
labeling. To benchmark TTU, we curate Video Instances,
States, and Transformation Annotations (VISTA), a dataset
including state graphs with 124 assembly and 132 disas-
sembly events across 50 sequences and over 26,000 frames.
TIPO achieves state-of-the-art tracking and state-graph ac-
curacy on VISTA, while operating 6× to 15× faster than
prior methods.

1. Introduction
Imagine someone preparing a Moka pot (Fig. 1, t1): they
unscrew the collector (t2), pull out the funnel filter (t3), and
set the components aside. In a matter of seconds, a single
object splits into three distinct parts. Modern visual founda-
tion trackers (e.g., SAM 2 [26], SAM 3 [6]) are built around
entity persistence: they follow objects through complex mo-
tion, but have no mechanism to revise identities when objects
physically divide or merge. As shown in the last row of Fig. 1
(SAM 2.1), the tracker either stubbornly preserves a single
ID across a split (t2) or arbitrarily drops fragmented parts
(t3, t4). Reassembling the pot (t4, t5) reverses the ambiguity:
should the tracker follow separate components or recognize
a unified Moka pot again? Yet the physical world is inher-
ently dynamic [30, 32], with objects routinely assembled,
disassembled, sliced, and folded [11, 15]. Maintaining pixel
tracklets is only half the challenge; dynamic environments
require a system that tracks objects through transformations,
describes the physical process, and updates identities and
part–whole relationships over time [19, 40].
To address this dynamic scenario, we formalize the task of
Transformation Tracking and Understanding (TTU). Given a
video and initial object prompts, a TTU system must (i) track
objects through assemblies and disassemblies, (ii) describe
the physical transformations, (iii) update tracklet IDs and se-
mantic labels, and (iv) keep track of how every object relates
to its parts over time. The output is a single, unified state
graph paired with object masks (Fig. 1, TIPO row). This
structured representation naturally enables several down-
stream applications: robots can verify step completion be-
fore planning actions [23], and AR assistants can monitor
current assembly states to guide users [24].
Prior work only partially addresses this task. Tubelet-
Graph [29] handles isolated split events but has three major
limitations for TTU (Fig. 1, second row). First, its search for
parts triggers only after track loss, missing the initial split
(t2) and detecting only later fragmentation (t3). Second, it
is restricted to disassemblies and cannot model assemblies,
causing identity switches (t4) and failing to resolve assem-
blies (t5). Lastly, tracking dense candidate regions across
the whole scene incurs severe overhead (0.2 FPS).
We take a fundamentally different approach. Our key
observation is simple: split and merge events are observable
in the current frame’s geometry. In Fig. 1, the collector’s
separation is instantly evident at t2 because the tracked mask
fragments into disconnected components. There is no need
to scan through the video to recover lost parts; the visual evi-
dence is already in the frame. Building on this, we introduce
Tracking Instances via Proposals and Overlaps (TIPO), a
training-free framework for TTU that explicitly models both
assemblies and disassemblies on top of foundation trackers.
At each frame, TIPO executes a generate–evaluate–judge
pipeline. It first generates split and merge state proposals by

Contributions. Our core contributions are: i) We formalize
the TTU task, extending prior tracking paradigms [29] to
model both object assemblies and disassemblies. ii) We pro-
pose TIPO, a training-free framework that replaces dense
spatiotemporal search [29] with per-frame geometric evalua-
tion of state proposals using mask-decoder priors, backed by
sparse MLLM validation and semantic labeling. iii) We cu-
rate VISTA, the first benchmark to provide comprehensive
state-graph annotations for both assembly and disassembly.
iv) We show through extensive experiments that TIPO es-
tablishes a new state-of-the-art for tracking and state-graph
accuracy, while running 6× to 15× faster than competitors.

Understanding Object Transformations. Recent literature
explores physical state changes in videos. Early work tar-
geted human-driven events in egocentric datasets [11, 15],
introducing benchmarks like VOST [30] and VSCOS [36].
Subsequent work pursued fine-grained understanding: Vi-
dOSC temporally localizes open-world transformations [32],
while SPOC spatially segments progressive changes [20]
for robotic manipulation [19]. Most recently, Sun et al. [29]
bridged state recognition and tracking via the Track Any State
task and VOST-TAS benchmark. Their TubeletGraph frame-
work tracks all candidate regions of the scene as tubelets
and, after a transformation, filters them with semantic and
spatial priors to decide which belong to the target. How-
ever, tracking the whole scene is computationally heavy, and
the framework is strictly limited to disassembly events. In
contrast, TIPO natively models both assemblies and disas-
semblies. By evaluating state proposals via per-frame spatial
overlap, we achieve accurate transformation tracking at a
fraction of the computational cost.
MLLMs for Understanding Object Transformations. Re-
cent MLLMs, including both closed-source models [1, 13,
22] and open-weight architectures [14, 25], have shown
strong capabilities in parsing long-context video, enabling
the grounding of dynamic physical properties, causality, and
structural changes [17, 39]. For tracking transformations,
prior work primarily used MLLMs as offline pseudo-label
generators for training [32] or as post-hoc captioners to ex-
tract textual descriptions from mask-contoured keyframes
[29]. In contrast, our pipeline integrates an MLLM to vali-
date state changes directly within the tracking loop, effec-
tively filtering out implausible false positives.

3.2. Our Approach: Tracking Instances via Propos-
als and Overlaps
Overview.
We introduce Tracking Instances via Proposals
and Overlaps. As shown in Fig. 2, TIPO is a modular
framework built on top of pre-trained segmentation trackers
and MLLMs, requiring no additional training.
We initialize the tracker and state graph G using the ini-
tial objects O0. At each frame It, the tracker extracts the
per-object masks Mt = {mi
t}i∈Ot. TIPO processes these
masks through four interconnected stages: (1) Component
Extraction & Object Mask Sampling decomposes the per-
object masks into spatially disjoint components and prompts
a mask decoder to extract per-component sampled masks.
(2) State Generation enumerates plausible combinations
of the spatially disjoint components (for disassemblies) and
sampled masks (for assemblies) to output a set of state pro-
posals. (3) State Evaluation scores the state proposals
using spatial overlap metrics applied to the sampled masks,
yielding evaluated state scores. Finally, (4) State Selection,
MLLM Validation & Graph Update filters the evaluated
state scores to query an MLLM, visually verifying the top-
scoring state proposal. If validated, the framework assigns
semantic labels and outputs the updated state graph G.

3.3. The VISTA Benchmark
Overview.
While prior benchmarks like VOST-TAS [29]
consider state changes, they annotate only the resulting parts
rather than modeling the transformation process itself. To
address this gap and rigorously evaluate TTU, we introduce
Video Instances, States, and Transformation Annotations
(VISTA). As summarized in Tab. 1, VISTA comprises 50
diverse sequences sourced from Assembly101 [27], IKEA
ASM [2], and custom recordings. It provides continuous,
dense segmentation masks paired with comprehensive state
graphs that explicitly link parts as they separate and merge
over time. By modeling multi-stage assemblies and disas-
semblies and more than doubling the annotated transforma-
tions of prior work [29], VISTA provides a highly realistic
testbed for dynamic environments (see Appendix C.1 for
the detailed dataset composition and Appendix C.2 for the
complete annotation protocol).

4.2. Ablation Study
Single-point prompts let SAM resolve ambiguity through
its own prior. A single point is inherently ambiguous: it
may belong to a sub-part, a part, or the whole object. SAM
resolves this by selecting the most plausible mask, revealing
which object it believes the point belongs to. Ensembling
these single-point decodings probes SAM’s zero-shot ob-
jectness prior without biasing the decoding. Adding points
removes this ambiguity: the decoder must cover all prompts,
and the mask collapses onto the sampled component. For
an occluded object, the decoded mask no longer extends
to other fragments, producing spurious disassemblies; for
assemblies, merged parts no longer yield overlapping masks.
As shown in Tab. 5, sampling K = 4 points drops TF1 by
11.7 with the MLLM Judge and 11.3 without it.
Both inner and external mask overlaps are strictly re-
quired to evaluate state proposals. In Eqs. (6) and (7), we
formulated Φin, Ψin and Φex, Ψex as the foundational metrics
for assessing assemblies and disassemblies. Tab. 5 demon-
strates the critical necessity of external recall (Φex, Ψex). Ex-
cluding these terms mathematically blinds the evaluation to
whether a sub-state incorrectly bleeds into external compo-
nents, causing performance to collapse by −17.7 TF1 points
for assemblies and −15.3 TF1 points for disassemblies. For
instance, a hand holding a pen splits its mask into two compo-
nents c1, c2 whose sampled masks both cover the whole pen.
Inner recall alone then scores the spurious split {{c1}, {c2}}
as high as the intact pen {{c1, c2}}, whereas Φex penalizes
the leakage of each part into the other.
Advanced reasoning models provide precise transforma-
tion classification. We evaluate the impact of the MLLM
Labeler in populating the final state-graph labels. As shown
in Tab. 6, while object naming accuracy (SO) remains rel-
atively stable across most models, action naming (SA) re-
quires deeper temporal understanding. GPT-5.4 and GPT-5.4
mini outperform the best local model by at least +9.8 SA
points, while GPT-5.4 delivers the highest overall semantic
accuracy S and is our default MLLM Labeler.
The MLLM Judge substantially improves spatial rea-
soning, though the Judge-free pipeline remains highly
competitive. The MLLM Judge acts as a final filter to verify
whether the highest-scoring state proposal actually occurred.
As shown in Tab. 2, integrating the judge yields a definitive
improvement of +8.0 to +9.1 TF1 points across trackers.
However, achieving over 33.0 TF1 relying solely on mask
overlap scoring shows that the Judge-free configuration suits
deployments with strict computational constraints.
5. Conclusion
We introduced Transformation Tracking and Understanding
to generalize video tracking to environments where objects
undergo structural changes, unifying assembly and disassem-
bly into continuous state graphs. To benchmark this task, we
curated VISTA, featuring 256 densely annotated, multi-step
transformations. We also proposed TIPO, a training-free
framework that replaces reactive spatiotemporal search with
continuous, per-frame structural evaluation and MLLM vali-
dation. By leveraging the zero-shot priors of modern foun-
dation models, TIPO achieves state-of-the-art tracking and
state-graph accuracy, operating up to an order of magnitude
faster than prior work. Our results demonstrate the effective-
ness of continuous structural modeling for tracking objects
in complex, real-world environments.

B. Extended Methodology and Implementation
This section expands upon the TIPO framework presented
in Sec. 3.2. We first provide additional details on the method
and report the exact prompts of the MLLM Judge and La-
beler. Then, we describe the selection of the hyperparame-
ters and the software and hardware specifications required
to reproduce our results.
B.1. Additional Method Details
Ensembled Mask Sampling Protocol.
As detailed in
Sec. 3.2, the framework extracts the sampled masks si
j by
prompting the visual decoder with a single point (K = 1)
drawn from within the spatial boundaries of each disjoint


component. Relying on a single random point coordinate can
introduce sampling variance. To ensure structural stability,
we perform this single-point query N = 32 times per compo-
nent and ensemble the outputs to form a single, highly robust
union mask si
j. During this ensembling process, we discard
any individual mask proposal whose predicted quality score
from the SAM decoder falls below τIoU = 0.8.
Tracker and Mask Decoder Modularity.
While Sec. 3.2
conceptually separates the Tracker and the Mask Decoder,
our experiments instantiate both using the same foundation
model (e.g., SAM 2.1 or SAM 3) to maximize inference
efficiency via feature caching. However, the framework
architecture is strictly modular, decoupling temporal tracking
from single-frame zero-shot segmentation. This flexibility
allows future deployments to pair a lightweight, tracking-
only model with an independent promptable mask decoder,
naturally accommodating advancements in either domain.
Algorithmic Bounds for State Generation.
As formu-
lated in Sec. 3.2, the State Generator enumerates all possible
physical configurations by computing the partition set Π(X)
of the tracked objects or disjoint components. Because the
cardinality of a partition set scales according to the n-th Bell
number Bn, an unconstrained search could lead to combina-
torial explosion. To ensure computational tractability in our
experiments, we set a configurable parameter that limits the
number of locally interacting items to |X| ≤7, which limits
the search space at B7 = 877 unique state proposals. To
further optimize assembly generation, we strictly pre-filter
the input space by excluding any objects whose sampled
masks do not spatially overlap. Additionally, to avoid very
small noisy components in the mask to interfere, we filter
each component with area smaller than 0.02% of the image
resolution, analogous to TubeletGraph [29]. The measured
runtime of state generation and evaluation up to this bound
is reported in Appendix D.3.
Crucially, this threshold only defines the maximum
degree of the state graph for a single transformation
event—meaning a maximum of seven parts can assemble
into one entity, or one entity can disassemble into at most
seven parts simultaneously. It does not limit the total num-
ber of independent objects the framework can continuously
track in the background, which can be arbitrarily high. The
value of 7 was simply chosen for our experimental setup
(in VISTA, the maximum in-degree for an assembly is 2,
and the maximum out-degree for a disassembly is 3) and
can easily be increased depending on available hardware and
application requirements.
B.2. MLLM Prompts
MLLM Judge Prompts.
The MLLM Judge serves as a
visual validator to confirm whether the proposed transforma-
tion reflects actual physical changes in the scene. To provide
the necessary temporal context, the model receives sparsely
sampled historical frames alongside the current frame over-
laid with proposed regions. A transformation is accepted into
the state graph only if the model explicitly outputs “YES”.
The exact prompts for assembly and disassembly validation
are detailed in Figs. 4 and 5, respectively.
MLLM Labeler Prompts.
Once a structural change is
validated, the MLLM Labeler is queried to populate the state
graph with explicit semantic labels. It assigns concise nouns
to the newly formed object states and a descriptive verb
phrase to the transformation edges. The exact prompts used
for assembly and disassembly labeling are detailed in Figs. 6
and 7, respectively.
B.3. Hyperparameter Selection
With the exception of the state selection threshold τ of TIPO
w/o Judge, which is selected by cross-validation (Fig. 8), no
hyperparameter was tuned on VISTA; we selected them
following standard or conservative values.
• τ: threshold used in the state selection (Sec. 3.2). In the
default configuration of TIPO (with MLLM Judge) we
use τ = 0.5. This comes from the interpretation of the
overlap scores as proxy measure for confidence in the
proposed state. A state that has overlap score below 0.5 is
considered spurious, therefore not considered in the final
selection. When removing the MLLM Judge (TIPO w/o
Judge configuration), the threshold is increased to τ = 0.8
to compensate for the lack of reasoning from the Judge, as
selected by the cross-validation protocol described below.
• K: number of points sampled to decode object masks
(Sec. 3.2). In Tab. 5 we ablated the choice of use K =
1. This is required to avoid a change in behavior from
SAM’s mask decoder. When using K ≥2 we remove
all the ambiguity in the mask decoding process. This
is a fundamental flaw because SAM will start decoding
mostly the specific component we prompt. In contrast,
when K = 1, SAM is forced to decode multiple masks
and score and select the most likely, solving occlusions,
and providing the informative signal needed to evaluate
quickly the proposed states.
• N:
number of masks sampled for component (Ap-
pendix B.1). This is set by default to N = 32, a fairly
large value to reduce the variance of the sampling process.
We observed that reducing N to 8, 4 and 1 has little ef-
fect on the results but for correctness we want to provide
a more robust sampled mask, specifically to avoid very
unlucky cases due to the random sampling of the decoding
points.
• τIoU: threshold used to ensemble decoded masks (Ap-
pendix B.1). This is set by default to τIoU = 0.8 reflecting
the convention of SAM to filter high quality masks.

B.4. Software and Hardware Specifications
All experiments, including pure tracking and MLLM infer-

All methods are implemented and evaluated using Python
3.12.13 and PyTorch 2.7.0 (CUDA 12.6). All open-weight

improve reproducibility, we set the decoding temperature to
0 for all LLMs and MLLMs whenever the model provider

C. The VISTA Benchmark
This section expands upon the VISTA benchmark intro-
duced in Sec. 3.3. First, we describe the composition of the
dataset. Then, we detail the custom annotation pipeline and
expert protocol utilized to guarantee high-fidelity ground-
truth state graphs for the Transformation Tracking and Un-
derstanding (TTU) task. Finally, we formalize the mathe-
matical definitions for our proposed tracking and state-graph
evaluation metrics.
C.1. Dataset Composition
VISTA comprises 50 video sequences collected from three
sources: 17 videos from Assembly101 [27], 8 videos from
IKEA ASM [2], and 25 custom recordings. The sequences
cover a diverse set of object categories, including generic
household objects (e.g., USB sticks, perfume boxes, moka
pots, mixers, remote controls, and digital cameras), IKEA
furniture (e.g., tables and drawers), and toy vehicles from
Assembly101 (e.g., bulldozers, garbage trucks, and excava-
tors), for a total of 218 unique object and part names. The
annotated transformations are described by 43 distinct ac-
tion descriptions, such as “disconnect”, “insert”, “mount”,
“pop”, “pull out”, “put”, “remove”, “screw”, “separate”, and
“unscrew”. All annotations have been manually checked and
validated by an expert annotator to ensure the temporal and
spatial validity of each transformation.

notator assisted by a Promptable Visual Segmentation tracker

to construct the ground-truth state graph. Each sequence was

Extending it past either bound would compare p against
objects that do not exist yet (e.g., inputs of g produced by

Since J is computed on union-level

metrics. We distinguish two types of identity switches. First,
a tracked object may drift to an untracked object. In this case,
the predicted mask no longer overlaps with its ground-truth
object, which is directly penalized by J and Jtr, and by TF1
whenever the object is involved in a subsequent transforma-
tion, since Tcheck requires an IoU > 0.5 for every involved
object. Second, two tracked objects may swap their identi-
ties. Since the union of their masks is unchanged, this event
is not captured by J . It is penalized by TF1 only if one of the
two objects is subsequently involved in a transformation and
the objects do not swap back before it occurs. Conversely,
if both objects are involved in the same transformation, the
internal bipartite matching of Tcheck absorbs the permutation
and the swap is not penalized.

D.5. Error Propagation Analysis
Due to TIPO’s online and streaming architecture it can be
subject to some irrecoverable errors in the state graph. We
want to assess whether the objects in the last frame of each
video sequence are correctly identified and segmented. This
proxy metric will indicate how well each method can recover
to a state consistent with the observed reality. On the last
frame of each video, predicted tracks and ground-truth ob-
jects are matched one-to-one with Hungarian matching on
mask IoU, and a match is counted as correct if its IoU is at
least θ. We report micro-averaged precision, recall, and F1
for θ = 0.5 and θ = 0.75. We compare against the base
tracker alone, which keeps the initial identities throughout
the video, and TubeletGraph [29].
As shown in Tab. 14, TIPO substantially outperforms
both baselines with either base tracker, improving over
TubeletGraph by +25.8 and +30.1 F1 points at θ = 0.5
with SAM 2.1 and SAM 3, respectively (70.1 vs 44.3 and
70.5 vs 40.4). The gain is mostly driven by precision (87.1
vs 43.3 and 93.4 vs 45.1). Since the baselines do not model
assemblies, they keep separate tracks for parts that have been
merged, which results in spurious objects on the last frame.
Moreover, the results of TIPO are almost unchanged un-
der the stricter threshold (−0.8 F1 with SAM 2.1, and 70.5
F1 at both θ = 0.5 and θ = 0.75 with SAM 3), whereas
TubeletGraph drops by −4.6 and −6.6 points, indicating
that the identities predicted by TIPO are also accurately seg-
mented. Notably, replacing SAM 2.1 with SAM 3 slightly
improves TIPO (70.1 to 70.5 F1 at θ = 0.5), while it de-
grades TubeletGraph (44.3 to 40.4) due to a drop in recall
(45.3 to 36.7). As discussed in Appendix E.3, SAM 3 loses
track of the parts after a transformation less frequently than
SAM 2.1, so TubeletGraph triggers fewer tubelet searches
and misses the newly formed objects. Conversely, recall (R)
is the critical aspect for all the methods, indicating a funda-
mental difficulty in tracking all the objects. We believe this
is mainly driven by small objects not picked up during the
transformations. We observe an example of this in Fig. 17.
To provide the most fair comparison with TubeletGraph
on the transformations it is designed to handle, we repeat
the evaluation on the disassembly only events of VISTA. As
shown in Tab. 15, TubeletGraph achieves a high precision
in this setting (95.1 and 89.8 with SAM 2.1 and SAM 3,
respectively), but recovers only 42.5% and 24.3% of the
objects on the last frame, as most of the parts produced
by the disassemblies are not detected. In contrast, TIPO
raises the recall to 56.4 and 55.8, more than doubling it with
SAM 3. With SAM 2.1, this comes at a lower precision
than TubeletGraph (90.3 vs 95.1), still resulting in a gain
of +10.6 F1 points (69.4 vs 58.8) at θ = 0.5. With SAM
3, TIPO also improves the precision (96.2), resulting in a
gain of +32.3 F1 points (70.6 vs 38.3). As in Tab. 14, the
results of TIPO are almost unchanged at θ = 0.75, whereas
TubeletGraph drops by −5.4 and −7.9 F1 points. Replacing
SAM 2.1 with SAM 3 reduces the recall of TubeletGraph
from 42.5 to 24.3, while the recall of TIPO is stable (56.4
vs 55.8).

This baseline tests the MLLM Judge

E.2. Full Comparison on VOST-TAS
Tab. 16 extends Tab. 3 with TIPO, vanilla SAM, and the
Agentic baseline on VOST-TAS [29]. Interpreting perfor-
mance on VOST-TAS requires understanding the critical
structural flaws of its evaluation protocol: first, VOST-TAS
annotates only the resulting parts at the end frame of each
transformation without modeling the state graph; second,
its primary metric HST is strictly recall-based and verifies
spatial overlap only at the final frame of the ground-truth win-
dow, completely blind to which input objects participated in
the event. As demonstrated in Tab. 3, this flawed formulation
heavily rewards blind over-prediction: the AlwaysPredict
baseline achieves the highest HST (13.9 and 15.7) simply
by firing spurious disassemblies at every mask split, despite
a disastrous temporal precision of 6.2–9.0 T TAS
P
.
This metric distortion explains the apparent disparity be-
tween TubeletGraph and TIPO under SAM 2.1. VOST was
specifically collected to induce catastrophic tracker failures
through extreme blur and heavy object deformation [30]. Be-
cause TubeletGraph detects transformations by interpreting
track loss as a structural split, it is artificially rewarded on
VOST when a weaker tracker (SAM 2.1) frequently drops
tracks, allowing its reactive tubelet search to trigger and
accumulate unpenalized recall credit on HST . In contrast,
TIPO evaluates geometry proactively and refuses to predict
transformations unless coherent disjoint components and
mask priors support them. On severe visual artifacts where
SAM 2.1 collapses, TIPO conservatively suppresses false
positives, yielding a low HST (2.2) solely because the metric
fails to penalize TubeletGraph’s hallucinated proposals.


USER
You are a video analysis agent. You control a video object tracker (SAM 3) through tools, and you can watch
the video through tools. Your job is to produce a complete, accurate record of the physical objects in a
video and of the transformations they undergo.
The task
The video shows physical objects being taken apart or put together. Think of an object as anything
that would move as a single unit if left alone: if you picked it up, everything that is part of it would
come along.
A transformation happens when this changes:
• DISASSEMBLY: one object becomes two or more objects.
• ASSEMBLY: two or more objects become one object.
Any change in what forms a unit counts, however small the part involved. Contact that does not outlast the
hand (holding, touching, occlusion) does not change what forms a unit.
You must produce:
1. A track for every object relevant to the task. A track is a segmentation mask on every frame where the
object exists. A track is an empty mask when the object is fully occluded, and has no mask at all outside
the object’s lifetime.
2. Every transformation, recorded at the frame where it happens (the first frame where the new state is
visible). Each transformation lists:
• the objects before it (the “before” objects);
• the resulting objects;
• the action.
The “before” tracks end at that frame, and each resulting object starts a NEW track at that frame, even
one that looks almost unchanged. Assembly/disassembly results are never continuations of an old track.
Together, the tracks and transformations form a graph of how objects change over time. Results of a
transformation are objects like any other: they can take part in later transformations (be split again,
or assembled with other objects), so keep following every object until the end of the video.
3. Concise names for every object (e.g. “pen cap”, “pen body”, “assembled pen”).
4. The action of each transformation, described with VERBS only. You may use several verbs (e.g. “unscrew,
pull apart” or “snap, press”).

TubeletGraph to detect the transformation. With SAM 3,
the rubber case is tracked robustly throughout the video, the
track loss occurs later on in the video and no transforma-
tion is ever triggered due to the proximity constraint not
being satisfied. Similarly, when the battery cover is removed,
SAM 3 keeps tracking it, so TubeletGraph cannot trigger any
transformation event by design. As a result, the state graph
collapses to a single node, producing a graph with no trans-
formations. The banana example exhibits the same behavior,
with all three peeling actions missed when using SAM 3.
This explains why TubeletGraph cannot benefit from the
stronger SAM 3, whereas TIPO translates the improved
tracking quality into more accurate state graphs.
E.4. Fully Open-weight Pipeline
In our default configuration, TIPO already employs an open-
weight MLLM Judge (Qwen 3.5 35B), while the MLLM
Labeler relies on the closed-source GPT-5.4. To provide a
fully accessible and reproducible pipeline, we replace the
MLLM Labeler with Qwen 3.5 35B, such that all the com-
ponents of TIPO, as well as the LLM evaluator (Gemma 4
31B), are open-weight models.
As shown in Tab. 17, the fully open-weight configuration
remains highly competitive. Compared to the default con-
figuration in Tab. 2, replacing GPT-5.4 with Qwen 3.5 35B
mainly affects action naming (SA), leading to a moderate
decrease of the overall score (25.5 vs 27.1 HF1 with SAM
2.1, and 23.2 vs 28.3 with SAM 3). Nevertheless, TIPO
still outperforms TubeletGraph by a wide margin, achieving
+22.9 and +23.2 HF1 points with SAM 2.1 and SAM 3,
respectively.
E.5. Tracking-only Comparison on VOST
To the best of our knowledge, no existing method natively
addresses the TTU task, and TubeletGraph is the only base-
line able to detect and track transformations, albeit limited
to disassemblies. While modern VOS models excel at propa-
gating pixel-level masks, they cannot generate state graphs
(TF1 = 0). To nonetheless benchmark pure tracking capabil-
ities, we compare TIPO against state-of-the-art VOS models
on the VOST [30] validation set in Tab. 18. SAM 3 remains
a highly competitive base tracker, and TIPO with SAM 3
achieves the best tracking quality overall, improving over
vanilla SAM 3 by +1.5 J and +3.4 Jtr points.

Then, we present additional qualitative results on VISTA
and analyze the failure cases of TIPO. Finally, we discuss
the current limitations of our approach.
Non-rigid Transformations.
While VISTA is designed
around assemblies and disassemblies of rigid objects and
does not specifically target non-rigid ones, neither the TTU
formulation nor TIPO is limited to rigid transformations.
Since state proposals are generated and evaluated directly
from the tracked masks, no assumption is made on the shape
of the involved objects. Fig. 15 shows an example from
VOST-TAS (sequence 3511_unscrew_jar_1) with SAM 3
as base tracker. TIPO correctly detects the cap being un-
screwed from the jar and, crucially, the sauce squeezed onto
the pasta as a new, highly deformable entity, whose mask
matches the ground truth with an IoU of 0.92 at the end of
the transformation. Notably, the masks of the cap and of
the sauce are temporarily lost during the video (e.g., while
the pasta is being stirred), yet both objects retain their iden-
tity once they reappear and no spurious transformation is
introduced. This confirms that TIPO does not require un-
interrupted tracks, since structural changes are evaluated
directly from the available masks rather than triggered by
track loss. The example also exhibits the failure modes dis-
cussed below. While the sauce is still flowing out of the jar,
the tracker repeatedly splits and re-merges the two masks,
producing four spurious transformations, and the MLLM
Labeler assigns generic names to some states (e.g., the jar is
initially labeled as a bottle cap).
Additional Qualitative Results and Failure Cases.
Fig. 16 presents additional qualitative results on VISTA,
illustrating successful multi-step assemblies, while Fig. 17
shows two disassemblies from the Assembly101 [27] subset.
While TIPO handles these sequences robustly, these exam-
ples also highlight specific failure modes stemming from our
direct reliance on upstream foundation models.
First, the MLLM Labeler occasionally struggles with
fine-grained semantics. Without external context (e.g., an
instruction manual), visually ambiguous components are
often assigned generic labels, such as identifying isolated
IKEA drawer parts simply as “blocks” (Fig. 16, top row).
Second, segmentation and tracking errors propagate di-
rectly into the state graph. As seen in Fig. 17, the base tracker
occasionally drops small parts (e.g., screws or wheels) im-
mediately following a disassembly. Furthermore, the bot-
tom row of Fig. 17 illustrates a hallucinated transformation
caused by a segmentation artifact: the tracker merges a screw-
driver into the toy truck mask. Because TIPO relies on these
masks to generate state proposals, this forces an unnecessary
structural update in the graph, even though the MLLM Judge
correctly recognizes them as separate entities.
Limitations.
As illustrated by the failure cases above, the
accuracy of TIPO is bounded by the quality of the base
tracker, the promptable mask decoder, and the reasoning
capabilities of the MLLMs. A part dropped by the tracker
or a segmentation artifact can lead to a missed or spurious
transformation, and visually ambiguous parts can receive
generic labels. Moreover, since TIPO operates in an on-
line, streaming fashion, the state graph is built causally, and


a transformation cannot be revised retroactively once later
frames are observed. This property is shared by prior work,
in fact, TubeletGraph [29] also processes videos in a stream-
ing fashion. Rather than recovering lost tracks, it tracks
all candidate regions of the scene as tubelets and decides
whether to include them in the state graph after a transfor-
mation, without ever correcting the transformations already
added to the predicted state graph. Crucially, in TIPO an
error in the state graph does not propagate to the rest of
the video. Although a wrong transformation remains in the
graph, subsequent state proposals are evaluated directly from
the available masks, allowing TIPO to return to a state con-
sistent with the observed scene. As shown in Fig. 15, objects
whose masks are temporarily lost retain their identity once
they reappear and, despite the four spurious transformations
caused by the flowing sauce, the objects present at the end
of the video are correctly identified. As shown in Tab. 14,
TIPO identifies the objects present on the last frame with
a precision of 93.4, outperforming TubeletGraph by +30.1
F1 points (Appendix D.5). The remaining gap lies in recall
(56.7), mostly due to small parts that are not picked up by
the tracker during a transformation. Promising directions to
address these limitations include an offline variant of TIPO
that exploits future frames to revise past transformations,
and the re-detection of parts missed during a transformation
to close the recall gap.
Finally, as discussed in Sec. 4.1, the direct dependence
on foundation models is also a fundamental strength. By
continuously evaluating structural proposals directly from
the raw tracker outputs, rather than relying on reactive and
failure-triggered search heuristics, TIPO is uniquely posi-
tioned to scale synergistically. As future vision and language
foundation models improve in robustness, our framework
will naturally and automatically inherit those gains without
requiring architectural changes.
